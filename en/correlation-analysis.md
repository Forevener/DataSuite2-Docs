# Correlation analysis

The **Correlation analysis** module measures the strength – and, where applicable, the direction – of relationships between pairs of variables. You can correlate all variables at once or pick specific subsets, choose from a broad library of association methods (classical correlations, rank-based, information-theoretic, and functional-dependence measures) or let the app pick automatically, and view results as a matrix, a long-format table, or a network visualization.

> **What is a correlation coefficient?** A number that describes how two variables move together. Classical correlations (Pearson's *r*, Spearman's ρ, Kendall's τ, and others) range from −1 to +1: +1 means perfect positive lockstep, −1 means perfect opposing movement, 0 means no pattern. In practice, you'll rarely see perfect values – something like 0.6 (e.g. height and weight) indicates a solid positive relationship, while 0.1 suggests the variables are barely related.

> **Signed vs. unsigned measures.** Some methods in this module are *unsigned* – they range from 0 to 1 and describe only the **strength** of association, not its direction. Cramér's V, the information-theoretic family (NMI, AMI, coherence, Theil's U), η², Hoeffding's D, Chatterjee's ξ, and distance correlation all fall into this camp. This is a feature, not a limitation: a sign is only meaningful when both variables are ordered, and many real-world associations (categorical data, non-monotonic shapes) have no natural "positive" or "negative".

1. [Select your variables](#setting-up) (or leave both lists empty to correlate [all available variables](./getting-started.md#choosing-variables))
2. Choose a [correlation method](#choosing-a-method)
3. Optionally [control for covariates](#controlling-for-covariates-partial--semi-partial-correlation) (partial / semi-partial) or [test for differences](#testing-differences-between-correlations) between cells
4. Adjust [display options](#display-options)
5. Click **Calculate correlations**

## Setting up

Two variable lists appear side by side:

- **Left variables** – appear as rows in the output matrix
- **Right variables** – appear as columns in the output matrix

Both lists are optional. Leave them empty to correlate all variables against each other. Select variables in only one list and the other defaults to all appropriate variables. Variables can be selected by clicking or drag-selecting across multiple items.

When you change the correlation method, the lists automatically filter to show only compatible variable types. A warning appears if no appropriate variables exist.

## Choosing a method

### Signed correlations (−1 to +1)

| Method | Symbol | Variable types | Measures |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pearson's r** (default) | r | Continuous + continuous | Linear association |
| **Spearman's rho** | ρ | Continuous or ordinal | Monotonic association (rank-based) |
| **Kendall's tau** | τ | Continuous or ordinal | Ordinal association (concordant/discordant pairs) |
| **Blomqvist's beta** | β | Continuous + continuous | Median-based quadrant association, robust to outliers |
| **Polychoric** | ρ<sub>poly</sub> | Ordinal + ordinal | Association between latent continuous distributions |
| **Tetrachoric** | ρ<sub>tet</sub> | Binary + binary | The 2×2 special case of polychoric – assumes binaries dichotomize latent normals |
| **Polyserial** | ρ<sub>ps</sub> | Continuous + ordinal | Assumes a latent continuous variable underlies the ordinal one |
| **Somers' D** | d<sub>yx</sub> | Continuous or ordinal | Asymmetric ordinal association, adjusts for ties |
| **Goodman & Kruskal's gamma** | γ | Continuous or ordinal | Ordinal association, ignores ties |
| **Point-biserial** | r<sub>pb</sub> | Continuous + binary | Equivalent to Pearson's r with a dichotomous variable |
| **Biserial** | r<sub>b</sub> | Continuous + binary | Assumes the binary variable is a dichotomized continuous variable |
| **Phi coefficient** | φ | Binary + binary | Based on a 2×2 chi-square test |

### Unsigned dependence measures (0 to 1)

| Method | Symbol | Variable types | Measures |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cramér's V** | V | Categorical or binary | Chi-square-based (Bergsma bias-corrected), works with more than two levels |
| **Correlation ratio (η²)** | η² | Categorical + continuous | Variance in one variable explained by grouping on the other (bias-corrected, ε²) |
| **Normalized mutual information** | NMI | Any + any | Shared entropy as a fraction of total entropy (symmetric uncertainty) |
| **Adjusted mutual information** | AMI | Any + any | MI corrected for chance overlap, normalized by the larger marginal entropy |
| **Rajski's coherence** | C<sub>R</sub> | Any + any | MI as a fraction of joint entropy |
| **Theil's U** | U | Any + any | Asymmetric – fraction of one variable's uncertainty explained by the other |
| **Hoeffding's D** | D<sub>H</sub> | Continuous or ordinal | Nonparametric test against any dependence alternative (assumes continuity – ties attenuate it) |
| **Chatterjee's ξ** | ξ | Continuous or ordinal | Asymmetric – detects any functional dependence, including non-monotonic (Y-ties take maximum ranks, X-ties are broken at random under a fixed seed) |
| **Distance correlation** | dCor | Continuous + continuous | Energy-based; zero only under full independence |

### Special

| Method | Symbol | Variable types | Measures |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mixed/Auto · observed scores** | varies | All | Picks the best observed-score method per pair (Pearson, Spearman, point-biserial, φ, Cramér's V, η²) |
| **Mixed/Auto · latent variables** | varies | All | Picks the latent-normal method per pair (Pearson, polychoric, polyserial, biserial, tetrachoric, Cramér's V, η²) |

> **Which method to pick?** Start with **Pearson's r** for continuous data – it's the most common and easiest to interpret. If your data is ordinal (e.g. Likert scales) or you're worried about outliers, use **Spearman's rho** or **Blomqvist's β**. If you suspect a non-monotonic (curved, cyclical) relationship, try **Chatterjee's ξ** or **distance correlation**. If you have a mix of variable types and don't want to think about it, **Mixed/Auto** handles everything – pick the **observed scores** flavour for general use, or **latent variables** when you need a coherent matrix for a factor model or SEM (see [Mixed/Auto selection logic](#mixedauto-selection-logic)).

> **Pearson vs. Spearman vs. Kendall:** Pearson measures *linear* relationships – it can miss a strong curved pattern. Spearman and Kendall both use ranks, so they capture any monotonic relationship (consistently increasing or decreasing). Kendall is more robust with small samples and has a more intuitive interpretation, but Spearman is more widely used and slightly more powerful with larger samples.

### Beyond classical correlations

Several methods in the table above aren't taught in introductory statistics courses but fill genuine gaps left by Pearson, Spearman, and friends. They fall into three loose families.

**Robust and variance-based.**

- **Blomqvist's β** – also called *medial correlation*. Counts how often both variables land on the same side of their respective medians, then rescales to a `[-1, +1]` range. Because it uses only the sign of each value relative to the median (not its magnitude), it's highly resistant to outliers. A useful sanity check when Pearson's *r* seems dominated by a few extreme points.
- **Correlation ratio (η²)** – the share of a continuous variable's variance that is explained by grouping on a categorical one. Mathematically equivalent to the `R²` of a one-way ANOVA. DataSuite reports the **bias-corrected** form, `ε² = 1 − (1 − η²)·(n − 1)/(n − k)`: the raw sample ratio is inflated by `(k − 1)/(n − 1)` under independence, so with many groups and a modest sample it can land in the *strong* band on pure noise – and it can even sit outside its own confidence interval, which is built for the population quantity. The symbol, the F test, the p-value and the Cohen bands are all unchanged; the only visible consequence is that the corrected value dips slightly below 0 when the groups genuinely don't differ. Use it when one side of the pair is truly categorical (not ordered) and the other is continuous – a case where Pearson simply doesn't apply. If you ask for η² on a pair where both variables are categorical, the result falls back to Cramér's V automatically – assigning arbitrary numeric codes to one side would make η² depend on the labelling, which isn't a property you want. For mixed continuous/categorical pairs, the categorical side is always the grouping factor and the continuous side is the value. This choice depends only on the variables' intrinsic types, not on which one you put as "left" or "right", so the η² matrix is symmetric in input order. η² is only meaningful when one side can act as a grouping factor, so when η² is selected the **Left variables** list offers only grouping-capable variables – categorical, or numeric with at most 10 distinct values – while the right list stays unrestricted; a pair of two high-cardinality continuous variables (which has no grouping factor) can't be formed. Use a correlation method (Pearson, Spearman) for two continuous variables instead.

**Information-theoretic.** All four are bounded `[0, 1]` and all share the same first step: discretize continuous variables into equal-frequency bins (the bin count follows a [configurable rule](#discretization-bins) – a cube root of N, `≈ ∛n`, by default – capped at the number of distinct values), compute the mutual information (MI) – how much knowing one variable tells you about the other, measured in *nats* (natural-log units; one nat ≈ 1.44 bits) – and then normalize in different ways.

- **Normalized mutual information (NMI)** – MI divided by the average of the two marginal entropies. Often called *symmetric uncertainty*. The safest default for "how much do these two variables share?"
- **Adjusted mutual information (AMI)** – MI with the amount expected by chance removed, then divided by the *larger* of the two marginal entropies: `(MI − E[MI]) / (max(H(X), H(Y)) − E[MI])`. Useful when variable pairs have very different numbers of categories, since unadjusted MI tends to inflate in high-cardinality settings. It is not NMI with a correction subtracted off – NMI divides by the *average* of the two entropies, so AMI sits below NMI for two reasons at once: the chance correction and the stricter denominator. Under independence AMI can land slightly below zero; the raw value is reported, and the unsigned visualizations clamp the display side at 0.
- **Rajski's coherence** – MI divided by the *joint* entropy. Stricter than NMI: approaches 1 only when the two variables are nearly identical (modulo relabelling).
- **Theil's U** – MI divided by the entropy of one specific side. **Asymmetric** – U(Y given X) ≠ U(X given Y), so the matrix is not symmetric. Read as "fraction of Y's uncertainty that knowing X would eliminate".

**General-dependence detectors.** The classical correlations all miss non-monotonic patterns (a U-shape gives Pearson ≈ 0). These three don't.

- **Hoeffding's D** – a classical nonparametric test statistic that is zero under independence and positive under any form of dependence. Sample D can dip slightly below zero under independence – that's the noise floor around the null mean of 0, not anti-dependence – and the raw value is reported so you can see it. The unsigned visualizations clamp the display side at 0. Note that D lives on a much smaller scale than the mutual-information measures (roughly −0.5 to 1, with strong dependence rarely above ~0.3), so it carries its own configurable strength bands rather than sharing the information-metric ones. **D assumes continuous data.** Both the statistic and its p-value table (Blum–Kiefer–Rosenblatt) are built for distinct values, and neither applies a tie correction, so on heavily tied or ordinal data D is *attenuated* and its independence test turns *conservative* – two identical 5-level ordinals return D ≈ 0.57, not 1, and the test's rejection rate at α = 0.05 falls to about 0.01 on 5-level data and to 0 on binary data. The [tie-burden check](#rank--ordinal-methods-tie-burden) flags this in the suitability column; when it fires, read D as a lower bound and prefer distance correlation or the mutual-information family, which handle discrete data natively.
- **Chatterjee's ξ** – introduced in 2021; reaches 1 when one variable is a perfect function of the other (even a wildly non-monotonic one) and 0 only under independence. **Asymmetric** – ξ(X → Y) tells you whether Y is a function of X, not the other way around. Like Hoeffding's D, ξ saturates only near a deterministic relationship and so sits well below the mutual-information scale for noisy data; it carries its own configurable strength bands rather than sharing the information-metric ones. **Ties in the first variable are broken at random**, so on tied data ξ is one draw from a small family of values the same data supports rather than a single determined number – on a five-level variable, three draws gave 0.267, 0.219 and 0.200. The draw is seeded, so your value is reproducible from run to run, and a caveat under the table fires whenever a cell's first variable actually carries ties. Only that direction is flagged: ξ is asymmetric, and it is the *first* variable's ties that the random ranking perturbs.
- **Distance correlation (dCor)** – energy-distance-based; zero *if and only if* the two variables are independent. Picks up any dependence a classical correlation misses, at the cost of being harder to interpret as "strength". The reported value is the square root of Székely & Rizzo's bias-corrected estimator, which targets dCor**²** – the square root puts it back on the same 0–1 scale as |r|, the scale the strength bands and the color ramp assume. The bias correction itself can dip slightly below zero under independence, so it is clamped at 0 before the square root; the displayed dCor never goes negative. On very large samples (above 5000 rows) the statistic is computed on a fixed random subsample – the full n × n distance matrices would exhaust browser memory – and the reported N is the subsample's.

> **When to reach for a general-dependence measure:** classical correlations all have one blind spot – they can't tell a strong U-shape or cyclic pattern apart from no relationship at all. If your scatterplot shows *structure* but Pearson and Spearman both come back near zero, Chatterjee's ξ or distance correlation will catch it. Hoeffding's D is the classical choice for hypothesis tests of "are these independent at all?"

### Directional (asymmetric) methods

**Somers' D**, **Theil's U**, and **Chatterjee's ξ** are *directional*: they answer "how well does X predict Y?" rather than "how related are X and Y?" The resulting matrix is **not symmetric** – the cell at row A, column B generally differs from the cell at row B, column A.

The convention used throughout this module is **row → column**: the value in cell `[row, col]` describes how well the row variable predicts (or explains) the column variable. A direction caption under the matrix reminds you of this whenever one of these three methods is active. The **Hide redundant values** checkbox is also automatically hidden – both triangles carry genuinely different information.

**Assumptions:**
- **Pearson's r** assumes both variables are continuous and roughly normally distributed, with a linear relationship between them. Violations (skewness, outliers, curvilinear patterns) can distort the coefficient.
- **Spearman's rho and Kendall's tau** only assume a monotonic relationship and ordinal-level data. No normality requirement – use these when Pearson's assumptions are violated. They do assume relatively few ties, though; heavy ties strain their p-values (the [tie-burden check](#rank--ordinal-methods-tie-burden) flags this).
- **Blomqvist's β** assumes continuous data but has no distributional requirements beyond a well-defined median – robust to outliers and heavy tails.
- **Polychoric, polyserial, and tetrachoric** assume the discrete variables reflect underlying continuous normal distributions. Polychoric is generally reasonable for Likert-type items with 4+ response categories; tetrachoric is the 2×2 special case – sensible when the binary split is *artificial* (an underlying continuum was dichotomized), less so when the variable is genuinely binary by nature. **Phi** is a safer choice for genuine binaries; reach for tetrachoric when you have theoretical reason to believe the binary masks a continuum.
- **Point-biserial and biserial** assume the continuous variable is normally distributed within each group defined by the binary variable. Biserial additionally assumes the binary split is artificial (an underlying continuum was dichotomized).
- **Phi, Cramér's V, and the mutual-information family** all rest on a contingency-table framework – expected cell frequencies should ideally be ≥ 5 per cell (for Phi and Cramér's V, the [expected-cell-count check](#contingency-table-methods-χ²-cell-counts) flags when they aren't, and the [Monte-Carlo p-value mode](#p-value-method-contingency-table-methods) is the remedy). Continuous variables are auto-discretized into equal-frequency bins (count `≈ ∛n`), while a declared-categorical variable is coded level-for-level with no such cap – so a high-cardinality categorical produces a very sparse table, where the mutual-information *coefficient* itself runs high, not just its p-value. With very small samples the binning is coarse either way, so read the metric as approximate.
- **η² (correlation ratio)** relies on the one-way ANOVA framework – roughly equal variances across groups and approximately normal within-group distributions help the F-based p-value but don't affect the η² statistic itself.
- **Hoeffding's D, Chatterjee's ξ, and distance correlation** are nonparametric and make essentially no distributional assumptions beyond independent observations.
- **All methods** assume independent observations – each row should represent a separate case, not repeated measurements from the same subject.

### Mixed/Auto selection logic

**Mixed/Auto** comes in two presets, picked from the method dropdown. Both choose a method per pair from the variables' types; they differ only in *how they treat ordered categories and binaries*:

- **Mixed/Auto · observed scores** keeps the robust, assumption-light measures that work directly on the values as observed (φ, point-biserial, Spearman).
- **Mixed/Auto · latent variables** returns the coherent latent-normal family (tetrachoric, biserial, polyserial, polychoric) – the *heterogeneous correlation* (hetcor) matrix you want when fitting a factor model or SEM on a mix of continuous, ordinal, and binary items, where every coefficient should estimate the correlation of the underlying continuous variables.

| Pair type | · observed scores | · latent variables |
|---|---|---|
| Continuous × continuous | Pearson's r | Pearson's r |
| Continuous × ordinal | Spearman's ρ | Polyserial |
| Continuous × binary | Point-biserial | Biserial |
| Ordinal × ordinal, ordinal × binary | Spearman's ρ | Polychoric |
| Binary × binary | Phi coefficient | Tetrachoric |
| Categorical × (categorical / binary) | Cramér's V | *(same as observed)* |
| Continuous × categorical | Correlation ratio (η²) | *(same as observed)* |
| Ordinal × categorical | Cramér's V | *(same as observed)* |

> **Observed or latent?** Reach for **observed scores** for general-purpose exploration and reporting – the coefficients describe the data you actually have, and φ / point-biserial / Spearman stay valid without assuming a normal continuum beneath each category. Reach for **latent variables** when the ordered/binary variables are *coarsened continua* and you need a coherent matrix to feed a downstream model (factor analysis, SEM): the latent family assumes a normal continuum under each ordered/binary variable and estimates *its* correlation, so a tetrachoric and a polychoric cell in the same matrix sit on the same scale. The trade-off is the [latent-normality assumption](#latent-methods-bivariate-normality) – check it before trusting a latent matrix.

> **Polychoric fits get slow on wide tables.** A polychoric estimate is a two-dimensional maximum-likelihood fit over the pair's thresholds, so its cost grows with the number of categories on each side, not with the row count. When a run would fit any pair with more than **20 levels** on a side – whether you picked **Polychoric** explicitly or the latent preset dispatched to it – an advisory names how many such pairs there are and what to expect: roughly 30 seconds per fit at 20 levels a side, about a minute at 40. It is a heads-up, not a block; the run proceeds either way. If the wait is unacceptable, treat the wide variable as continuous (Pearson / Spearman) or collapse its categories first.

Categorical (non-binary) combinations resolve identically under both presets, and **asymmetric methods (Somers' D, Theil's U, Chatterjee's ξ) are never auto-picked** by either – they require explicit user intent, since mixing a directional measure into an otherwise symmetric matrix would break the visual contract.

## Checking assumptions

Click **Check assumptions** to run a comprehensive diagnostic pass and surface a single *Correlation assumptions* card. Unlike **Calculate correlations**, it does **not** limit itself to the method you selected: for every pair of selected variables it runs *every check applicable to that pair*, so a failed primary test immediately shows which alternative methods would fit. Because the pass is comprehensive, **explicit methods and Mixed/Auto follow the same path** – the selected method only decides which method is marked *primary* per pair. It runs independently of **Calculate correlations**, so you can check the diagnostics without (or before) computing the matrix, and it is **advisory**: it never changes the coefficients you asked for – it tells you whether each method is on solid footing for your data and, when it isn't, which alternative fits.

> **No method is assumption-free.** Pearson's r leans hardest on distributional assumptions, but the rank methods are not immune – they assume relatively few *ties*, and that assumption breaks down for coarse ordinal or heavily repeated data. The comprehensive pass surfaces all of these at once.

When a grouped [comparison structure](#comparison-structures) (**Between groups** or **Equality across groups**) is active with a **Compare across** variable, the assumption pass follows the analysis: every check runs **separately within each group**, on the same row subsets the per-group analysis uses, and the card shows one titled section per group with a note on top. The pooled rows are never checked – a pooled Mardia, RESET, or Levene verdict can fail (or pass) purely from between-group mean shifts, certifying an analysis that never runs. The compare-across variable itself is excluded from the checked pairs (it is constant within each group), while *which* checks apply to which pair is still decided on the full data, so every group section carries the same rows and columns.

### The per-pair method-suitability matrix

The card leads with a matrix – one row per variable pair, the pair-level checks as columns, and a **Suitable methods** column that synthesizes them. A column whose every cell would read `–` is dropped: an all-continuous run has no contingency tables, no latent pairs and no groups, so those three columns never appear at all.

- **N** – the number of complete (listwise) cases for the pair. When it falls below the [small-sample advisory floor](./settings.md#assumption-advisory-thresholds) (30 by default), the **Suitable methods** column adds a non-dropping *small sample* advisory – below that soft floor every coefficient is unstable and every test under-powered, whichever method you use.
- **Bivariate normality**, **Linearity (RESET)**, **Homoscedasticity (Breusch-Pagan)**, **Influential points (Cook's D)** – the [OLS diagnostics](#ols-diagnostics-pearson), shown for continuous pairs (where Pearson applies). Each is a *Pass* / *Fail* / *N/A* verdict; a `–` means the check doesn't apply to that pair.
- **Equal group variances** – the [group-comparison Levene check](#mean-comparison-methods-point-biserial-and-η²), shown for the mean-comparison pairs the point-biserial / η² methods fit (one continuous variable across the groups of a discrete one); `–` elsewhere. *Fail* means the within-group variances differ.
- **Adequate expected counts** – the [χ² cell-count](#contingency-table-methods-χ²-cell-counts) verdict, shown for contingency-table pairs (Phi / Cramér's V) and for discrete × discrete latent pairs (polychoric / tetrachoric), whose observed zero cells drive the [latent boundary-risk advisory](#latent-methods-bivariate-normality); `–` elsewhere.
- **Latent bivariate normality** – the [latent-normality goodness-of-fit](#latent-methods-bivariate-normality), shown for the pairs the latent methods fit (polychoric / tetrachoric / polyserial / biserial); `–` elsewhere. *Fail* means the assumed underlying bivariate normal is rejected.
- **Dependence beyond monotone (dCor screen)** – the [non-monotonic dependence cross-check](#non-monotonic-dependence-dcor-cross-check), shown for continuous/ordinal pairs; `–` for nominal pairs. *Fail* means the data carry dependence beyond the best monotone fit – structure no linear or monotonic measure can see. The word *screen* is deliberate, and the column header carries a tooltip saying so: the probe is fitted on the same data it then tests, so its p-value is approximate rather than calibrated at α.
- **Suitable methods** – the synthesis. Every method whose data types fit the pair is listed, unless one of its assumption checks fails:
	- A **failed check drops the method** from the suitable list and shows it parenthetically with the reason – e.g. *Pearson's r — not met: linearity, influential points*. Only the checks that bite the *coefficient* drop a method.
	- **A check that bites only the inference is an advisory, not a drop.** A rejected bivariate normality or heteroscedasticity leaves Pearson listed with a muted *(… rejected – affects the interval and p, not the coefficient; select Bootstrap CI)*; sparse expected counts leave φ / Cramér's V listed with *(sparse expected counts – the analytic χ² p is unreliable; set the p-value method to Monte Carlo)*. In both cases the coefficient is sound and the note names a remedy the module actually ships – a [bootstrap interval](#confidence-intervals), or the [Monte-Carlo χ² route](#p-value-method-contingency-table-methods).
	- When a check *applies* to a method but **couldn't run** (a batch error, or too little data to assess it), the method stays listed but carries a muted *suitability check could not be run* note – so an unassessed method reads distinctly from one that genuinely passed, instead of being silently presented as fully suitable.
	- **Rank methods stay listed even under heavy ties**, carrying a caveat – ties strain the p-value, not the estimate. Spearman and Kendall get *use permutation p-values* (permutation stays valid under ties, where the tie-corrected approximation and the exact distributions don't). Goodman & Kruskal's γ and Somers' D carry **no** tie caveat at all: they are built for tied ordinal data and their standard errors are tie-aware, so ties aren't a defect for them.
	- **Latent methods** (polychoric / tetrachoric / polyserial / biserial) stay listed when their types fit, even when their normality goodness-of-fit fails, but pick up a *bivariate normality rejected – estimates may be biased* advisory – the latent model is often the only option for the pair's types.
	- **Mean-comparison methods** (point-biserial / η²) stay listed; when the group variances differ they pick up a non-dropping *unequal group variances – affects the pooled t/F behind the p and the interval, not the coefficient; select Bootstrap CI* advisory.
	- **Information-theoretic methods** (NMI, AMI, coherence, Theil's U) are listed on essentially every pair as a distribution-free fallback – they auto-discretize any data type, so they carry no pair-level check and are never dropped.
	- **Robust and shape-agnostic methods** (Blomqvist's β, Chatterjee's ξ, Hoeffding's D, distance correlation) are likewise listed whenever their data types fit – they make essentially no distributional assumptions and are never dropped. Two of them still take a tie caveat, though: **Blomqvist's β**, whose median-quadrant counts degrade when many values coincide, carries an *interpret with caution* flag; and **Hoeffding's D**, which assumes continuity outright, carries *D is attenuated and its test is conservative* – a different warning, because permutation p-values are not a route it has.
	- The **primary** method – the one you selected, or the best-fitting method for the pair under Mixed/Auto – is marked.

Every verdict cell carries a tooltip with the numbers behind it – hover a *Pass* / *Fail* to see the underlying statistic and p-value (the OLS diagnostics' Mardia skewness/kurtosis, RESET F, Breusch-Pagan statistic, and max Cook's D with the count of points past the size-adjusted 4/(n−p) cutoff, plus max leverage with the count of points past the 2p/n leverage cutoff; the latent check's χ², df, and p; the dCor cross-check's Spearman ρ, residual dCor, and p – plus the subsample size when the probe ran on a large-n subsample; the χ² cell counts, including the observed-zero-cell count; the Levene F).

> **Note:** the size-adjusted Cook's cutoff shown in the tooltip is `4/(n−p)` (4 over the residual degrees of freedom, `p` = the regression's parameter count), the count of points past it is advisory only – the *Influential points* verdict itself uses the hard `D > 1` rule.

> **Why advisory rather than automatic?** Switching methods per pair would leave you reading a matrix where different cells use different measures – not comparable to one another. Keeping a single method across the matrix and flagging where it's strained preserves comparability while still steering you toward an alternative when the data warrant it. A failed linearity or normality check points to Spearman (it captures any monotonic relationship); heavy influential points point to Kendall (the most outlier-resistant).

### OLS diagnostics (Pearson)

For **every continuous pair** – regardless of the method you selected – the card runs Pearson's OLS-based diagnostics, so the matrix tells you whether Pearson would be sound even when you've asked for a rank method:

- **Bivariate normality** – Mardia's test of joint (skewness + kurtosis) normality of the pair.
- **Linearity (RESET)** – Ramsey's RESET test; a failure flags a curved relationship that Pearson would understate.
- **Homoscedasticity (Breusch-Pagan)** – whether the scatter is even across the range; a failure means the relationship is tighter in some regions than others.
- **Influential points (Cook's D)** – whether any single observation is strong enough (Cook's D > 1) to drive the result. The matrix reports the verdict; the supporting size-aware (4/(n−p)) and high-leverage (hat value > 2p/n) counts still inform it.

> **Both regression directions are checked.** RESET, Breusch-Pagan, and Cook's D are fit on a regression of one variable on the other, so their result depends on which variable is treated as the outcome – but correlation is symmetric and has no such direction. To keep the verdict from hinging on an arbitrary choice, each of these three checks is run in both directions and the pair is flagged if *either* direction violates the assumption.

> **Which failures drop Pearson.** Only **linearity** does – under curvature *r* is no longer the linear-association summary its label claims. **Bivariate normality** and **homoscedasticity** never drop it at any sample size: neither changes the coefficient, only how wide its interval and how exact its p-value should be, so a rejection adds the non-dropping *rejected – affects the interval and p, not the coefficient; select Bootstrap CI* advisory instead. **Influential points** sit between the two. A dominating observation does move *r* itself, so the advisory bucket's "not the coefficient" wording would be false for it – but `D > 1` is a magnitude rule rather than a test, too crude to strike a method over. It therefore gets a caveat of its own: Pearson stays listed and stays primary, with *influential points – these move r itself, not only its interval; select Bootstrap CI, or Kendall's τ, which resists them* beside it. Kendall is named unconditionally because it accepts every pair Pearson does; Blomqvist's β resists influence equally well but needs strictly continuous columns, so naming it could point at a method the pair cannot use.

> **Large samples.** Above n = 5000, the rejection-based checks (bivariate normality, linearity, homoscedasticity) no longer count against Pearson at all – at that size they reject almost any real data while Pearson's central-limit-backed inference is at its most robust, so a "failure" would mislead. This mirrors the n > 5000 cap on the marginal Shapiro-Wilk test. Cook's-D influence is exempt from the cap and still raises its caveat, since a single dominating observation is a problem at any sample size. One nuance survives the cap: when RESET fails at this size, Pearson stays listed but picks up a non-dropping *nonlinearity flagged – RESET is oversensitive at this N; inspect a scatterplot* advisory, since detected curvature – unlike a cosmetic normality rejection – is worth a look at any N.

> **A handful of failures is what chance looks like.** Every pair-level check that runs at your [assumption-test α](./settings.md#assumption-test-significance-level) fires once per assessed pair, so even on data that satisfies every assumption, some rejections are expected. Before counting them against a method, the card compares the observed number of failing pairs against a binomial upper bound at that check's per-pair failure rate. Mardia's bound uses `1 − (1 − α)²`, since skew and kurtosis are two statistics either of which can reject; RESET, Breusch-Pagan, the latent goodness-of-fit and Levene each use plain α (RESET and Breusch-Pagan do run in both regression directions, but the two orientations are fitted on the same rows and reject together, so their honest rate is a single test's). When the failures don't outrun that bound, every raw rejection still shows in its column – nothing is hidden – but a muted note under the matrix explains that they are no more numerous than chance produces and are therefore not counted against any method. Influence is excluded from this accounting: Cook's `D > 1` is a magnitude rule, not a test at α, so a binomial bound wouldn't describe it. OLS rows past the large-n cap are excluded too, since they never demote a method anyway.

When [control variables](#controlling-for-covariates-partial--semi-partial-correlation) are active, the OLS-family checks (and the marginal-normality table below) are computed on **residuals** after regressing out the controls – the normality/linearity/homoscedasticity assumptions behind a partial coefficient live in the residualized relationship, not the zero-order one – and the [beyond-monotone (dCor) cross-check](#non-monotonic-dependence-dcor-cross-check) is likewise run on control-residualized data, so every residualized check describes the same conditional relationship the reported partial coefficient does. A note under the matrix discloses it. Control variables themselves are excluded from the matrix axes – as in the main matrix, they appear only as covariates, never as subjects of an assumption check. In **semi-partial** mode only one side of each pair is residualized, so the two orderings are genuinely different estimates – both are shown in the main matrix, and the residualization-sensitive checks diagnose each accordingly: the per-pair matrix carries **two rows per pair**, one for each residualized side (labelled *(variable residualized)*), and the marginal-normality table tests each variable both residualized and raw.

A companion **Marginal normality (Shapiro-Wilk)** table appears below the matrix – one row per variable in a continuous pair, testing whether each variable's values are individually normally distributed. Alongside the W statistic and its verdict, each row reports moment-based shape and a concentration read:

- **Skewness** – asymmetry of the distribution. 0 is symmetric; positive means a long right tail, negative a long left tail.
- **Excess kurtosis** – tailedness relative to a normal (which sits at 0). Positive means heavier tails / a sharper peak, negative means lighter tails / a flatter top.
- **Max value share** – the largest share of observations held by any single value (the same concentration statistic the tie table reports).
- **Adequate spread** – the range-restriction verdict: *Fail* when the max value share exceeds the [range-restriction advisory threshold](./settings.md#assumption-advisory-thresholds) (0.5 by default), otherwise *Pass*. A judgment threshold, not a formal test.

The moments are computed even when N is too large for Shapiro-Wilk (> 5000, where the W column reads *N/A*), so a high-N row still carries a shape read instead of going blank. The diagnostics use the same active-filter sample as the correlations themselves. When any variable is range-restricted, a note explains that a dominant value or floor/ceiling pile-up attenuates correlations through it.

> **Reading the distribution diagnostics.** Shapiro-Wilk's W-verdict is sample-size sensitive – at large N it flags departures too small to matter, and at small N it misses real ones. The skewness and excess kurtosis put a number on *how* non-normal the data are, so you can tell a cosmetic *Fail* (W rejected, but |skew| ≈ 0 and excess kurtosis ≈ 0) from a substantive one. Max value share catches a separate problem the normality test won't: a variable can pass normality yet still be range-restricted by a single dominant value, which shrinks the correlation regardless of shape.

### Rank & ordinal methods (tie burden)

**Spearman**, **Kendall**, **Goodman & Kruskal's γ**, and **Somers' D** work on ranks or ordered categories, so they make no normality demands – but they do assume relatively few *ties* (observations sharing an identical value). When ties are heavy, the asymptotic formulas these methods use to compute p-values become unreliable. A variable-level **Tie burden** table below the matrix reports one row per variable in a rank-applicable pair:

- **N** – the number of non-missing observations for the variable.
- **Distinct values** – how many different values appear; a small count relative to N signals coarse, tie-prone data.
- **Tie correction Σ(t³−t)/(n³−n)** – the tie term itself, summed over the tied groups (`t` = the size of each group of identical values). This is the exact quantity that enters the rank statistics' variances – Spearman's tie-corrected SD, Kendall's τ<sub>b</sub> denominator – so it measures the tie burden the *formulas* actually feel. It is 0 with no ties and approaches 1 as a single value takes over. With `k` roughly equal levels it sits near `1/k²`, so a balanced 5-point Likert scale reads ≈ 0.04 while a lopsided binary reads far higher.
- **Max single-value share** – the largest share held by any single value. This surfaces a dominant category (e.g. a floor or ceiling pile-up) even when the overall tie correction looks moderate.
- **Adequate spread** – the range-restriction verdict on the max single-value share: *Fail* when one value holds more than the [range-restriction advisory threshold](./settings.md#assumption-advisory-thresholds) (0.5 by default), otherwise *Pass*. (The same threshold and check the marginal-normality table applies to continuous variables.)
- **Acceptable ties** – the tie-burden verdict: *Fail* when the tie correction exceeds the [heavy-ties advisory threshold](./settings.md#assumption-advisory-thresholds) (0.10 by default), otherwise *Pass*. This is a judgment threshold, not a formal test. The default is anchored on the `1/k²` rule above: it flags three or fewer effective levels and any strongly piled-up binary or ordinal variable, while a balanced Likert scale passes.

When any variable trips the tie threshold, a note recommends switching to **permutation-based** p-values (the [P-value method dropdown](#p-value-method-pearson-rank-and-ordinal-methods) for Spearman/Kendall), which don't lean on the tie-sensitive asymptotic approximation – Spearman's tie-corrected *t* / AS89 approximation degrades under heavy ties, and Kendall's exact test isn't available once ties are present at all. The note explicitly exempts **γ** and **Somers' D**, whose tie-aware standard errors are built for this data. A separate note fires when any variable is range-restricted – a dominant value attenuates the correlation itself, independent of the tie burden.

> **Ties strain the p-value, not the coefficient.** Spearman's rho and Kendall's tau stay well-defined under ties – their formulas apply a tie correction to the statistic itself. What suffers is the *significance test*: with many ties, the reference distribution is no longer the smooth one the closed-form p-value assumes. Read a heavy-tie flag as "the coefficient is fine, but double-check the p-value."

### Contingency-table methods (χ² cell counts)

**Phi** and **Cramér's V** measure association in a cross-tabulation, and their significance test is a χ² test – which relies on a large-sample approximation that breaks down when the table's *expected* cell counts are small. For each contingency-table pair the card builds the table, computes the expected count of every cell under independence, and applies **Cochran's rule**: the pair fails when any expected count falls below 1, or more than 20% of cells fall below 5. The matrix's **Adequate expected counts** column reports that verdict, on the same complete-case sample the correlations use. The same pass also counts the table's *observed* zero cells (reported in the cell's tooltip) and additionally runs over discrete × discrete latent pairs, where observed zeros matter for a different reason – the [latent boundary risk](#latent-methods-bivariate-normality) below.

When the rule is violated, φ and Cramér's V are **not** dropped from the **Suitable methods** column – the sparse table breaks the χ² approximation behind the *p-value*, not the coefficient. Instead the column adds a muted *sparse expected counts – the analytic χ² p is unreliable; set the p-value method to Monte Carlo* advisory, pointing at the [Monte-Carlo p-value mode](#p-value-method-contingency-table-methods) the module ships for exactly this case.

> **Why expected, not observed, counts?** The χ² statistic compares observed counts to the counts you'd expect under independence, and its reference distribution is only approximately χ² when those *expected* counts are reasonably large. A cell can have zero observations yet a healthy expected count (or vice versa) – it's the expected values that govern whether the approximation holds. A simulated (Monte-Carlo) p-value sidesteps the approximation entirely by generating tables with the observed margins and counting how often they reach the observed statistic.

### Information-theoretic methods (discretized grid)

NMI, AMI, Rajski's coherence and Theil's U all read a discretized table rather than the raw columns (see [Discretization bins](#discretization-bins)), so they inherit the same sparse-table problem the contingency-table methods have – except that here it inflates the **coefficient itself**, not only the p-value. The pre-flight therefore builds each pair's grid at the rule the run will actually use and reports what it found.

This check is deliberately **not a verdict**: it adds no *Pass* / *Fail* column to the matrix. It reports the realized grid and the smallest expected cell count under independence, and when that count falls below 5 – Cochran's floor, the same one the χ² check uses – the pair's **Suitable methods** cell picks up a muted clause naming the grid and the count, and suggesting you collapse categories or reduce the bin count. There is no traffic light because there is no threshold at which the measure stops being valid; the grid is a choice you make, and the number tells you what it cost.

> **Why the grid is measured, not calculated.** Equal-frequency binning keeps tied values in the same bin, so a tie-heavy variable realizes *fewer* bins than the rule asked for – a `nbins = 5` request on a column that is 50/30/20 tied lands 3 bins. Reading the requested count instead of the realized one would fire the advisory on exactly the data it is meant to describe, so DataSuite discretizes the pair for real and counts what came out. The advisory and the sparse-table caveat under the results are the same statistic on the same grid, so the pre-flight cannot warn about a table the results card then calls fine. On the default all-continuous run at n = 200 the cube-root rule gives a 6×6 grid with a smallest expected count of **5.12** and the advisory stays silent; the same data under Rice gives 12×12 at **1.125**, and both the advisory and the results caveat fire together.

### Latent methods (bivariate normality)

**Polychoric** (two ordinal variables), **tetrachoric** (two binary), **polyserial** (one continuous, one ordinal), and **biserial** (one continuous, one binary) all estimate the correlation of a pair of *latent* continuous variables assumed to lie beneath the observed categories – and that estimate is only valid when those latent variables really are **jointly bivariate normal**. Unlike the rank or contingency-table assumptions, this one bites the *coefficient itself*: if the latent normal model is wrong, the estimate is biased, not just its p-value. For each latent-applicable pair the card runs a **χ² goodness-of-fit test of underlying bivariate normality** on the same complete-case sample the correlations use:

- The test reuses the likelihood-ratio χ² that `polycor` (`polychor` / `polyserial`) already computes when it fits the pair – so the check is the estimator's own internal test, not a separate model.
- **Fail** (p < your assumption-test α) means the data are inconsistent with a bivariate-normal latent structure; the latent estimate should be read with caution.
- A **saturated table reports *N/A*, never a *Pass*.** A 2×2 tetrachoric fit (and any table with zero residual degrees of freedom) has nothing left to test – the model fits perfectly by construction, so there is no evidence either way.

When a pair fails, the **Suitable methods** column keeps the latent method listed but adds a *bivariate normality rejected – estimates may be biased* advisory. Unlike the OLS and χ² checks, a failed latent-normality test does **not** drop the method: the latent model is frequently the only one whose data types fit the pair, so the matrix flags the strain rather than leaving you no candidate.

> **Why doesn't this drop the method?** For two ordinal variables there is no assumption-free alternative that estimates the same latent association – dropping polychoric would leave the cell empty. The advisory is the honest middle ground: the estimate may be biased, but it is still your best available read, and a strongly non-normal latent structure is the signal to corroborate it with a model that doesn't assume normality (e.g. a rank measure on the same pair).

> **Empty cells in a 2×2 get a continuity correction.** A tetrachoric estimate on a 2×2 table with an *empty cell* but non-zero margins drifts to the boundary, returning an unreliable |ρ| ≈ 1. DataSuite adds the standard **+0.5 correction** to a zero cell of a **2×2 table only** – matching `lavaan`'s `zero.add = c(0.5, 0.0)` default, which adds 0.5 for a 2×2 and **0** for anything larger. On a wider r×c polychoric table the same fill biases ρ̂ badly downward (a 7×7 Likert pair at n = 60 with a true ρ of 0.85 estimates 0.86 uncorrected but only 0.61 corrected) while an empty cell there doesn't pin the estimate the way it does in a 2×2 – so it isn't applied. The correction is a no-op when no cell is empty, and the coefficient, its bootstrap CI and the latent-normality goodness-of-fit check all read the same table, so they can never describe different fits.

> **A boundary-pinned fit is seeded, not swapped.** Polychoric and tetrachoric coefficients are estimated in two steps (thresholds first, then ρ) with a delta-method standard error – fast, and the source of the **SE** column and the analytic CI. On strongly-associated tables that optimizer, started from ρ = 0, can pin at the boundary and return ρ ≈ 0.9999 with an infinite variance – a blank SE, CI and difference test beside a near-perfect coefficient. DataSuite therefore **seeds the fit with `polycor`'s own cheap two-step estimate**, which lands the optimizer on the right value directly: a 44/6/6/44 table returns ρ = 0.9298 with SE 0.0376 rather than pinning, matching to five decimals what a full maximum-likelihood refit would give. Only if a fit still comes back pinned or with a non-finite variance is the pair refit by **full maximum likelihood**, as a last resort – so every cell in the matrix carries the same estimator, and at lower cost than before.

> **The reported p-value tests ρ = 0, not the fit.** Polychoric and tetrachoric coefficients take their p from a **1-degree-of-freedom likelihood-ratio test** against independence – the fit's own deviance measured against the independence deviance on the same thresholds. Polyserial and biserial take theirs from `cor.test` on the continuous side against the numerically-coded ordinal one, which tests the same null. Both routes are boundary-safe: a Wald test built on the delta-method standard error breaks down as |ρ̂| approaches 1 – on a perfectly-associated 2×2 table it would report p ≈ 0.43 for an association that is as strong as it can be – while these stay monotone in the association all the way to the boundary. The **SE** column and the analytic CI still come from the delta method, so a very strong association can show a tiny p beside a wide interval; that is the delta method's limit near ±1, not a contradiction.

The correction mitigates the boundary drift but doesn't remove it, so sparse tables also get their own flag: when a discrete × discrete latent pair's contingency table has any **observed zero cell** (or fails Cochran's rule), the **Suitable methods** column adds a non-dropping *sparse or empty cells – estimates may sit near the ±1 boundary* advisory, and the **Adequate expected counts** tooltip reports the observed-zero count.

### Mean-comparison methods (point-biserial and η²)

**Point-biserial** (one continuous variable, one binary) and **η²** (one continuous variable across the groups of a categorical one) are mean-comparison measures in disguise: each summarizes how far a continuous variable's mean shifts between the levels of a discrete one. That makes them Pearson-/ANOVA-style measures, and they inherit the **two-/k-group comparison assumptions** – the same ones a t-test or one-way ANOVA rests on. For each such pair the card runs two diagnostics on the same complete-case sample the correlations use:

- **Within-group normality** – a **Shapiro-Wilk** test of the continuous variable *within each group* of the discrete variable. A companion **Within-group normality (Shapiro-Wilk)** table below the matrix reports one row per (variable × group): the group's N, the W statistic, its p-value, and a *Pass* / *Fail* / *N/A* verdict. (A group with fewer than 3 values, or more than 5000, is *N/A* – too small to test, or large enough that the test trips on trivial departures.) A rejection only counts against point-biserial / η² when **two** conditions hold: the rejecting group is smaller than the [small-sample advisory floor](./settings.md#assumption-advisory-thresholds) (the *t* and *F* these measures rest on are robust to non-normality above it), **and** more groups reject than chance produces at your assumption-test α. Every rejection still shows in the table; when the pair verdict discounted them, a note under the table says so.
- **Equal group variances** – a **Brown-Forsythe Levene test** (the median-centered, robust variant) of whether the continuous variable's spread is the same across groups. This is the matrix's **Equal group variances** column.

When the variances differ (Levene *Fail*), the **Suitable methods** column keeps point-biserial / η² listed but adds a non-dropping *unequal group variances – affects the pooled t/F behind the p and the interval, not the coefficient; select Bootstrap CI* advisory, which also names **Somers' D** where the grouping side is numeric enough for it to apply. As with the latent check, this flags strain rather than removing the method – the coefficient is still what it claims to be; it is the pooled-variance t/F behind its p-value and its analytic interval that weakens.

> **Why are these checked like a t-test?** Point-biserial *is* the correlation you get by coding a two-group variable 0/1 and running Pearson – algebraically identical to the two-sample t-statistic. η² is the share of a continuous variable's variance explained by group membership – the effect size of a one-way ANOVA. So "is each group roughly normal, with comparable spread?" is exactly the right question to ask of them, even though they're reported as correlations.

### Non-monotonic dependence (dCor cross-check)

Pearson, Spearman, and Kendall only see *monotonic* structure: Pearson measures straight-line association, the rank methods measure consistently-increasing-or-decreasing association. A relationship with **structure beyond a monotone trend** – a U-shape, a cycle, an oscillation riding on a trend – can leave all three misleadingly small even though the variables are tightly dependent. For **every continuous/ordinal pair** the card probes for exactly the dependence a monotonic coefficient cannot see:

- Both variables are converted to **ranks**, and an **isotonic (monotone) regression** of one rank on the other – rising or falling per the pair's Spearman sign – absorbs the entire monotone component of the relationship: everything a Spearman-family measure could possibly capture.
- **Distance correlation** (`energy::dcorT.test`) then tests the **residuals** of that fit. Distance correlation is zero *only* under full independence, so whatever dependence remains after the best monotone fit – curvature, cycles, fans – shows up here (the probe needs n ≥ 4; the t-approximation is most reliable around n ≥ 20).

The pair is flagged **Fail** when the residual distance correlation is both significant (p < your assumption-test α) and at least *negligibly* strong (the residual dCor – square-rooted onto the same scale the main coefficient uses – reaches the lowest information-metric band, 0.05 by default). Requiring the *magnitude* to clear the band, not just the test to reach significance, keeps the flag from firing on trivial artifacts in large samples – where the distance-correlation test rejects almost any departure from independence. When the probe can't run (n < 4, a constant variable) the cell is *N/A*, never a spurious *Pass*. Above n = 5000 the probe runs on a fixed random subsample – building the full n × n distance matrices would exhaust browser memory – and the tooltip reports the subsample size. In a [partial or semi-partial](#controlling-for-covariates-partial--semi-partial-correlation) run, the probe's two input columns are first residualized on the control variables with a rank-based (Spearman) partialling – matching how the partial coefficient beside it is formed – so the cross-check tests dependence *beyond the monotone trend of the conditional relationship*, not the zero-order one.

When a pair fails, the **Suitable methods** column adds an advisory pointing to shape-agnostic measures – **distance correlation**, **mutual information**, or **Hoeffding's D** – which capture the dependence a monotonic coefficient understates. The cross-check never *drops* a method: a monotonic coefficient isn't wrong, it just answers a narrower question than your data may demand. The advisory is skipped when your selected method is itself an omnibus dependence measure (the mutual-information family, Chatterjee's ξ, Hoeffding's D, distance correlation) – those already see this structure.

> **It is a screen, not a calibrated test.** The isotonic fit is estimated from the same data whose residuals are then tested, so the reported p-value is approximate rather than exact at your α. The column header (**Dependence beyond monotone (dCor screen)**) says so in its tooltip, and a note under the matrix repeats it. Read a flag here as a prompt to open the scatterplot, not as evidence at a stated significance level.

> **Why test residuals instead of comparing dCor to Spearman?** Either number alone is ambiguous, and even reading them together fails when both components are present: a moderate trend *plus* a strong oscillation gives a solid ρ **and** a solid dCor, and no comparison of the two reveals the extra structure. Removing the monotone fit first and testing what remains isolates exactly the dependence the monotonic lens cannot see – however strong the trend sitting on top of it.

### Control-variable collinearity (partial mode)

When a [partial or semi-partial correlation](#controlling-for-covariates-partial--semi-partial-correlation) is active, the assumptions card adds a **Control-variable collinearity** section. Partialling on near-collinear covariates is unstable – when two controls (or a control and one of the target variables) are almost the same variable, the residualization that removes their shared influence becomes numerically ill-conditioned, and the partial coefficient swings wildly on small data changes. The check correlates the continuous controls and target variables and flags any pair that **involves at least one control** (control–control or control–target) whose `|r|` exceeds **0.90**; a high target–target correlation doesn't destabilize the conditioned estimate (and already shows in the main matrix), so it isn't flagged:

- **No pair flagged** – the section reports the largest `|r|` among those control-involving pairs, so you can confirm the control set is well-separated.
- **One or more pairs flagged** – each offending pair is listed with its `r`; consider dropping or combining the redundant control before trusting the partial estimate.

> **Why only continuous variables?** Collinearity is a property of numeric predictors; an arbitrary integer coding of a multi-level categorical would make the correlation depend on the labelling. Categorical controls fall outside this particular diagnostic.

## Controlling for covariates (partial & semi-partial correlation)

A **Control for** list appears under the variable selection when the chosen method is **Pearson**, **Spearman**, or **Kendall** – these are the only methods that support partialling in this module. Pick one or more numeric covariates from the list; the module will compute the correlation between each pair *after* removing the linear (or rank-based) influence of those covariates.

> **Why partial correlation?** Two variables can correlate strongly only because a third variable drives both. Controlling for that third variable strips out the shared influence and shows whether any direct relationship remains. The classic example is the ice-cream–drowning correlation: control for temperature and the correlation vanishes. Partial correlation is also how you detect *suppression*: cases where a relationship is hidden by a confounder and becomes visible (or even reverses) once the confounder is held constant.

### Partial vs. semi-partial

Once at least one covariate is selected, a mode radio appears:

- **Partial (residualize both)** – the canonical partial correlation: removes the covariates' influence from *both* variables, then correlates the residuals. Symmetric. Use this when you're asking "what is the direct relationship between X and Y, holding Z constant?"
- **Semi-partial (residualize row variable only)** – removes the covariates' influence only from the **row variable** (Variable 1, the left-list variable), then correlates the result with the raw **column variable** (Variable 2). **Asymmetric** – the cell at row A / column B differs from the cell at row B / column A. Use this when you want to know how much *unique* variance the row variable contributes to the column variable above and beyond the covariates.
- **Semi-partial (residualize column variable only)** – the mirror: residualize the **column variable** (Variable 2) only.

In matrix display, semi-partial output behaves like the other asymmetric methods – both triangles are populated with genuinely different numbers, the redundancy hider is forced off, and a *Direction: row → column* caption is added.

The semi-partial **p-value** is the increment test – "does the row variable add anything over the covariates?" – which is the same null the full partial correlation tests, so a semi-partial cell and the matching partial cell report the identical p (and the same p a regression of the column variable on the row variable plus the covariates would give for that coefficient). Only the *coefficient* and its *interval* differ between the two modes. Its **interval**, in turn, uses its own variance rather than the partial one: a semi-partial coefficient has a different sampling distribution, so analytic confidence intervals and [TOST](#negligible-correlation-margin-tost) p-values for **Pearson** semi-partials use the Aloe & Becker (2012) delta-method standard error. **Spearman** and **Kendall** semi-partials have no analytic variance – their confidence interval is available only under *Bias-corrected percentile bootstrap* (it shows `–` under *Analytic*, since there is no closed-form to fall back on and a bootstrap CI is never substituted silently), while their TOST runs on a bootstrap standard error computed on demand whenever you set a [negligible-correlation margin](#negligible-correlation-margin-tost) – even under *Analytic* or *None*. The bootstrap is run only for those explicitly-requested results, never as a hidden stand-in for an analytic CI.

### Zero-order companion

Partial correlation is fundamentally a *comparative* statement, so each cell and each long-format row also reports the **zero-order** coefficient – the same correlation computed *without* the covariates, on the same complete-case sample. It's labeled with a `₀` subscript (e.g. `ρ₀` for Spearman). Read the result as "controlling for Z, the X–Y relationship moved from ρ₀ = 0.61 to ρ = 0.18" – the gap tells the confounding (or suppression) story directly.

### Sample size, missing data, and scatterplots

- Pairwise deletion now spans *all* involved variables – the pair (X, Y) and every covariate – so an observation missing any one of them is dropped. The minimum needed is `k + 3` complete cases, where *k* is the number of covariates.
- The control list shows only numeric variables. Categorical covariates aren't supported in this pass; you can numeric-code them upstream if needed.
- Control variables are excluded from the matrix axes – they appear *only* as covariates, never as subjects of correlation.
- Scatterplots under **Pearson** and **Spearman** partials become **residual–residual scatterplots**: each axis shows the residuals of that variable after regressing out the covariates (for *semi-partial* modes, only the residualized side is replaced). The OLS line on a partial-Pearson residual scatter then has the slope of the partial regression coefficient, and the `r` value displayed in the corner matches the partial coefficient in the table. Its analytic confidence band uses the partial degrees of freedom (`n − 2 − k`, with `k` the number of covariates), so the band width matches the partial coefficient's own inference rather than a naive simple-regression band. Axes are relabelled with a `| covariates` suffix. **Kendall** partial scatterplots are *not* residualized – the raw points and LOWESS smoother depict the **zero-order** relationship, since there is no exact rank-based analogue to OLS residualization that would reproduce the partial-τ value. The corner annotation frames this honestly: the partial τ is labelled as conditioned (`τ | covariates`, and in semi-partial mode `τ (variable | covariates)`, naming the one side that was actually adjusted) and the **zero-order τ₀** that the smoother actually shows is listed beside it, so the marginal-vs-partial gap – the confounding story – reads straight off the plot.

> **A caveat on partial Spearman and partial Kendall.** Both rank-based partials are implemented via `ppcor` – partial Spearman as a Pearson partial on rank-transformed data (the residual scatter therefore shows residuals of **ranks**, with axis labels suffixed `(ranks) | covariates`), partial Kendall as a closed-form expression on pairwise τ's. These are common operational definitions but not the only ones in the literature; if your community uses a different convention (e.g. the Kendall partial τ derived from concordant/discordant triples), keep that in mind when comparing across tools.

## Testing differences between correlations

A correlation by itself often isn't the question – the real question is whether two correlations *differ*. Does X relate to Y more strongly than to Z? Did the X–Y correlation change across groups? Is the whole pattern of correlations the same in two samples? The **Test for differences between correlations** card lets you answer those questions directly, with the right test for each comparison structure.

> **Why a special test?** You can't just look at two correlations side by side and call them different – sampling variability matters, and so does whether the two coefficients share a sample. Two r's from the **same sample** are statistically dependent (they share rows and often share variables), so the test must account for that dependence. Two r's from **different samples** are independent and need a different test. The card picks the right family for you based on the comparison structure.

### Comparison structures

Pick one of six structures from the **Comparison structure** dropdown – grouped into **Pairwise** (one comparison per cell pair) and **Joint** (a single omnibus statistic for the whole matrix). The options that don't apply to your current matrix shape are hidden automatically – for example, *within each row variable* needs at least two column variables.

| Family | Structure | What it tests | Sample | Test family used |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pairwise | **Within each row variable** | All pairs of column-cells against each other (same row anchor) | Single | Williams' T2 (dependent overlapping) |
| Pairwise | **Within each column variable** | All pairs of row-cells against each other (same column anchor) | Single | Williams' T2 (dependent overlapping) |
| Pairwise | **Against a reference cell** | Every cell in the matrix against one chosen reference cell – data-driven (**strongest |r|** / **weakest |r|**) or a **pre-specified cell** | Single | Williams' T2 when cells share a variable; Steiger's (1980) modification of Dunn & Clark's z (Olkin–Siotani covariance) when they don't |
| Pairwise | **Between groups** | For each cell, the same pair across two groups defined by a grouping variable | Two (independent) | Fisher *z*, or Wald *z* on the analytic SE for the latent/ordinal methods – both for independent correlations |
| Joint | **Pattern equality (single sample)** | One equality **Pattern** over the whole matrix – all correlations equal, or equal within each row / column variable – as a single joint statistic | Single | Steiger pattern-equality χ²; a bootstrap-calibrated Wald under a bootstrap CI |
| Joint | **Equality across groups** | Whole correlation matrix is the same in every group | k groups (independent) | Jennrich (1970) trace χ² for k = 2; Wald χ² on Olkin–Siotani covariance at the pooled R̄ for k ≥ 3 |

The **Between** and **Omnibus across groups** structures require a **Compare across (variable)** dropdown – pick the categorical variable that splits the sample into groups. The other four structures use the full sample. A variable with many distinct values – a continuous column or an ID picked by mistake – would create one near-empty group per value, and every group costs its own correlation batch; when the level count runs high the analysis warns you to check that the grouping variable is really categorical and reminds you that the wait scales with it.

> **Reference cell pickers.** The **Reference cell** dropdown has two groups. *Picked from the data* offers **Strongest |r|** (the largest absolute coefficient in the matrix) and **Weakest |r|** (the smallest); both break ties lexically by `(row, col)` name so the pick is deterministic across runs. *Pre-specified cell* lists every cell the current row × column selection produces, so you can name the comparison anchor in advance. Prefer a pre-specified cell whenever you can: a reference chosen *because* it was the strongest or weakest coefficient is chosen from the same data it is then compared against, which biases every difference away from it and makes the p-values read optimistically. A data-driven pick therefore carries an explicit optimism footnote under the table; a pre-specified one is captioned *(pre-specified, …)* and carries no such warning. If the cell you pre-specified turns out to be unusable – not part of the current matrix, or one whose correlation errored or came back non-finite – the card names that cell and says which of the three it was, instead of reporting an empty comparison.

> **Pattern (joint, single sample).** When the structure is *Pattern equality (single sample)*, a **Pattern** dropdown appears naming the constraint set the joint test evaluates: **All correlations equal (compound symmetry)** – today's default and, for a square symmetric matrix, the classic "every off-diagonal correlation is the same" hypothesis; **Equal within each row variable** – each row's cells must be equal to one another, rows free to differ; **Equal within each column variable** – the column-wise mirror. The three coincide whenever one axis is contained in the other – on a square symmetric matrix all three are the identical test, arrived at by different routes – and genuinely diverge only when neither axis contains the other. A single-anchor option is therefore hidden both when it would generate no constraints on the current matrix and when it would merely restate compound symmetry, so the dropdown never offers you the same test under three different hypotheses. Whichever pattern you pick, the card states its H₀ in words and reports the constraint count as the test's `df`.

> **Jennrich test variant.** The k = 2 across-groups omnibus uses Jennrich's canonical 1970 statistic. Its χ² can differ from tools that implement a deviating variant (notably `psych::cortest.jennrich`): in simulation the canonical form holds its nominal α and is more powerful, while the variant over-rejects when variables are strongly intercorrelated.

### Method coverage

- **Closed-form path** – covers **Pearson**, **Spearman**, **Kendall** (including their partials), and **point-biserial**. Closed-form variance is canonical for Pearson; Spearman, Kendall, and point-biserial use it as a plug-in approximation (for point-biserial the dichotomous margin makes the Fisher-z variance depend on the group split – Tate 1954 – so the caveat applies especially at extreme splits), and partials use it with `df = n − k − 3` (`n − k − 4` for Kendall; Wilcox 2009, §10.11).
- **Between-groups Wald path** – in the **Between groups** structure only, the six analytic-SE methods (**polychoric**, **tetrachoric**, **polyserial**, **biserial**, **Goodman & Kruskal's γ**, and **Somers' D**) also get a closed-form test. Independent samples share no rows, so the difference variance is just the sum of each cell's own Wald SE and `z = (r₁ − r₂) / √(se₁² + se₂²)` – reusing the same per-cell delta-method SE behind their [analytic CIs](#confidence-intervals) (Somers' D runs each direction of the ordered grid separately). Their *dependent*, same-sample comparisons (row-wise, column-wise, reference) have no closed-form cross-sample covariance and stay bootstrap-only.
- **Bootstrap path** – covers most other methods, including the asymmetric ones (Somers' D, Theil's U, Chatterjee's ξ – handled via an ordered pair grid so each direction gets its own distribution). It runs **only when Confidence intervals is set to *Bias-corrected percentile bootstrap*** – its resampling cost makes it an explicit opt-in, never a silent substitute. Under *Analytic* or with CIs off, only the closed-form-supported methods produce a difference test – Pearson / Spearman / Kendall / point-biserial in every structure, plus the six Wald-SE methods in the *Between groups* structure (above); any other coefficient shows no test and an inline hint to switch **Confidence intervals** to *Bias-corrected percentile bootstrap* (see [Reading the comparison table](#reading-the-comparison-table)). Single-sample structures share a row-index draw across all pairs per iteration so within-sample dependence is preserved; between-group draws are independent per group. That shared draw is also what lets the single-sample **Pattern equality** structure keep a joint test here: the same constraints go into a Wald statistic whose covariance is estimated from the resampled coefficients, referred to its own bootstrap distribution instead of a χ² tail (see [Omnibus fallback](#omnibus-fallback)). That covariance is only as good as the number of draws behind it: against a nominal 5% the joint test rejects about 8% of the time at the default 100 replications and about 6% at 400 or more, so raise **Bootstrap replications** before reading its p-value near a threshold.
- **Semi-partial mode** always routes through the bootstrap, since the closed-form Δr variance formulas are derived for symmetric (zero-order or canonical-partial) coefficients.
- **Mixed/Auto** has no diff test – the per-pair method dispatch can't be folded into a single comparison statistic. The card shows a warning if you try.

### Dependent-test variant

When two correlations come from the *same* sample – the row-wise, column-wise, reference and pattern-equality structures – their difference test has to account for the covariance between them, and that covariance is evaluated at an average of the two coefficients under H₀. Which average is a genuine choice, so the comparison card exposes it as a **Dependent test** dropdown:

- **Back-transformed average Fisher z (better under non-normality)** – the default. The two coefficients are averaged on the Fisher-*z* scale and mapped back, `tanh((atanh r₁ + atanh r₂)/2)`. For overlapping cells (the two correlations share a variable) this is Dunn & Clark's *z* as modified by Hittner, May & Silver (2003); for non-overlapping ones it is Silver, Hittner & May (2004), which is Steiger's test at the other average.
- **Raw average of the two coefficients (classic)** – the textbook pair: **Williams' T2** for overlapping cells and **Steiger's (1980)** modification of Dunn & Clark's *z* for non-overlapping ones, both evaluated at `(r₁ + r₂)/2`.

The dropdown appears for every dependent structure – including pattern equality, whose component tests and ACAT fallback run the same pairwise comparisons – and is hidden for the two cross-group structures, whose samples share no rows. It is a single paired choice rather than four separate options because the *reference* structure mixes overlapping and non-overlapping cells in one table, so the choice has to name a pair, not a test.

> **The two are closer than the literature suggests.** The back-transformed pair is the default on the strength of its reported robustness to non-normality, but measured here it does not pull ahead: at n = 60 over 4000 replicates under the null, Williams and Hittner reject at **.0500 / .0503** on normal margins and **.1052 / .1050** on a mildly skewed one – indistinguishable – while at n = 20 with ρ = .8 and skew the classic pair is marginally *better* (.119 vs .136). Under heavy skew both closed forms are badly liberal (about .23 at a lognormal margin); that is the [bootstrap path](#method-coverage)'s territory, not either formula's. Treat the dropdown as a reporting choice you can defend either way rather than a correctness fix. One structural note: the covariance denominator can go non-positive at high |ρ| under skew, and a cell that hits it declines to test rather than reporting a statistic built on a negative variance (measured at under 0.1% of replicates).

### Omnibus fallback

The pattern-equality and across-groups omnibus tests need an admissible covariance matrix, and can be denied one in more than one way: a small sample or sparse group sizes leave it rank-deficient, and the pattern-equality test evaluates its covariance at the H₀-restricted matrix – each constraint class collapsed to its mean – and that averaging can leave it inadmissible at any sample size. Under a bootstrap CI the single-sample pattern-equality test builds that covariance from the resampled coefficients instead, so there it is denied one only when too few replicates survive or the resampled constraints are collinear; the across-groups omnibus has no resampled counterpart and goes straight to the combination below. When that happens, the orchestrator falls back to a **Cauchy combination (ACAT, Liu & Xie 2020)** of the component tests – robust to arbitrary dependence between the components, at the cost of being less powerful than the proper joint test when the joint test is available. The output table labels which one was used. Across groups the components it combines are the per-cell homogeneity χ²s, where each group enters a cell once; the redundant pairwise rows (each group appearing in one comparison per other group) are combined only on the bootstrap path, which has no homogeneity test.

### Reading the comparison table

For the four pairwise structures (row-wise, column-wise, reference, between), the output card shows one row per comparison with these columns:

- **Pair A** and **Pair B** – the two cells being compared (variable names). The *reference* structure replaces Pair A with a banner above the table and shows only the **Compared cell**. The *between* structure replaces them with **Variable 1** and **Variable 2** – plus **Group A** and **Group B** columns when three or more groups are compared; a two-group comparison folds the group names into the coefficient headers instead (see below), so those two columns fall away.
- **r (A)** and **r (B)** – the two coefficients (or just **r** in *reference* mode). In a two-group *between* comparison the headers name the groups directly – e.g. **r (north)** and **r (south)** – which is what lets the separate Group A/Group B columns drop.
- **95% CI (A)** and **95% CI (B)** – each coefficient's own confidence interval, shown when **Confidence intervals** is set to *Analytic* or *Bias-corrected percentile bootstrap* (the percentage tracks your [confidence-level setting](./settings.md#confidence-level)). These are the per-cell CIs from the main matrix itself – under a bootstrap CI that is the cell's own pairwise-complete resample, not the difference test's separate whole-frame one, so one quantity never appears twice with two different bounds on the same card. In *reference* mode the reference cell's interval moves to the banner above the table and the column shows only the compared cell's CI; in a two-group *between* comparison these headers carry the group names too.
- **p (A)** and **p (B)** – each coefficient's own two-sided significance (its p-value against zero), so you can tell whether each correlation is individually significant, not only whether the two differ. They belong to the matrix's significance family, not the difference-test family, so the [comparison adjustment](#p-value-adjustment-for-comparisons) never touches them – but they follow the [adjustment display mode](./settings.md#multiple-comparison-adjustment): in *replacement* mode each column shows the same batch-adjusted value the main matrix shows for that cell (in a *between* comparison that's each group's own correlation family – adjusting within a group matches what you'd get filtering to that group and running the matrix alone, and the header tooltip says so), while *addition* mode keeps them raw. A two-group *between* comparison names the groups here as well (**p (north)**, **p (south)**); *reference* mode shows only the compared cell's **p**, with the reference cell's own p in the banner (which follows the same display mode).
- **N (A)** and **N (B)** – in a *between* comparison, each group's own pairwise-complete sample size, named after the groups just like the coefficient headers. A Δr between two groups of wildly different size is a different claim from one between two equal groups, and the per-group N is what tells them apart.
- **N** – in the three single-sample structures (row-wise, column-wise, reference), the sample size the difference test actually ran at: the rows complete on *every* variable the comparison involves. Williams' T2 and Steiger's z are derived for one sample complete on all of them, so under the default pairwise deletion this sits below either cell's own n – and it appears nowhere else on the card. The **95% CI (Δ)** is built at the same n, so the interval and the p-value can never disagree about how much data stood behind them. The bootstrap path carries no such n and the column drops there.
- **Δr** – the difference r(A) − r(B). In *reference* mode the header spells the direction out as **Δr (reference − cell)**, so the sign can't be read backwards once Pair A has moved into the banner.
- **95% CI (Δ)** – a confidence interval for the difference itself, shown alongside the others when CIs are on. *Analytic* uses Zou's (2007) MOVER method (each coefficient's Fisher-*z* CI combined with their estimate-correlation); *Bias-corrected percentile bootstrap* reads **bias-corrected (BC)** percentile quantiles of the resampled Δr (the same median-bias recentering the per-cell bootstrap CIs apply). **This is the interval to read for the comparison** – if it excludes 0 the difference is significant at the matching level, and it stays meaningful even where no closed-form statistic exists.
- **Statistic** – the difference-test statistic with significance stars. When a single table runs one test throughout, its symbol labels the **column header** – **t** for Williams' T2, **z** for Steiger Z / Fisher z / the between-groups Wald z – rather than repeating in every cell. The *against a reference cell* structure can mix both families in one table (Williams' T2 where the cells share a variable, Steiger Z where they don't); the header then reads the neutral **Statistic** and each cell carries its own t/z label. The **Bias-corrected percentile bootstrap** path carries its inference through the **Δr** interval and p-value instead, so it drops the Statistic and df columns entirely.
- **df** – degrees of freedom, shown only for Williams' T2. The *z*-based tests (Fisher z, Steiger Z, the between-groups Wald z) and the bootstrap path carry no per-row df, so the column is omitted rather than filled with `–`.
- **p (Δ)** – the difference-test p-value (labeled to set it apart from the per-coefficient **p (A)** / **p (B)** above), plus an adjusted p in a separate column if [adjustment](./settings.md#multiple-comparison-adjustment) is enabled in *addition* mode (otherwise the adjusted value replaces the raw one). On the **bootstrap** path this p-value is **bias-corrected (BC)** with the same `z₀` recentering as its **95% CI (Δ)**, so the two never disagree at the boundary – `p (Δ) < α` exactly when the α-level CI (Δ) excludes 0; the closed-form paths carry their own statistic's p.

In a *between* comparison across **three or more groups**, a small **Homogeneity across all groups** table appears *above* the pairwise rows: a χ² testing H₀ *the correlation is the same in every group*, pooled over each cell's per-group coefficients and weighted by each one's own variance (so Kendall and the Wald-SE methods use their own standard errors, and Pearson reduces to the textbook Fisher-*z* form). It is the omnibus over the pairwise rows beneath it – read it first, then the rows for where the difference sits. One χ² per cell is a family of its own, so these rows carry their own [adjustment](./settings.md#multiple-comparison-adjustment), separate from the pairwise rows below them. It is a closed-form test, so it is skipped on the bootstrap path: an explicit bootstrap request never gets a closed-form answer.

The row-wise and column-wise structures get the same protection per anchor. Above their pairwise rows sits an **Equality within each row (column) variable** table – one Steiger pattern-equality χ² per anchor, testing H₀ *that variable's correlations are all equal*, with its `df` the number of independent constraints and its **N** the rows complete on the whole matrix. Only anchors carrying at least two comparisons get a row; with a single comparison the joint test would just restate the pairwise one. Like the between-groups homogeneity block it is adjusted as its own family and skipped on the bootstrap path.

For omnibus structures, the table reports the test label, **N** (the sample size the statistic rests on – the rows complete on every variable in the matrix, listed per group for the across-groups tests), the statistic, df, and the p-value, with the tested **H₀ stated in words** underneath. The bootstrap-calibrated single-sample test carries no **N** column – its resample spans every row and each pair drops its own missing values – and instead names underneath how many usable replicates its covariance and p-value rest on. Below it, a **Component comparisons** sub-table lists the individual pairwise comparisons the omnibus summarizes, so a significant joint result can be traced to the cells driving it. When the [fallback](#omnibus-fallback) fires, the note names *why* the joint test wasn't used – no closed-form joint test for this method, bootstrap CIs active (across groups), a missing/errored cell or a non-positive-definite matrix, too few complete cases, an undeterminable effective sample size, no equality constraints formed from the selected cells, a singular constraint covariance matrix, or – on the bootstrap path – a missing resampled matrix or draws too thin to carry the test.

In the across-groups structure, a group whose correlation matrix can't be assembled at all is **dropped rather than allowed to cancel the test** – the remaining groups are compared as long as at least two survive, and a note names the ones left out. A **Per-group coefficients** table sits above the component comparisons: one row per cell, with each group's coefficient and its n side by side. The joint test asks whether those matrices are equal, and this is where you read them – the component rows spread each cell over one comparison per pair of groups, so with more than two groups the matrices themselves are otherwise impossible to reconstruct by eye.

A footnote under the table identifies which test family was used (Williams' T2, Steiger's z for non-overlapping cells, Fisher z, the between-groups Wald z, or the bias-corrected percentile bootstrap with the replication count) and surfaces any plug-in caveats that apply (the Spearman/Kendall/point-biserial plug-in approximation, partials' df adjustment – `n − k − 3`, or `n − k − 4` for Kendall – and semi-partial bootstrap routing).

Cells that have no closed-form test show `–` in the **Statistic** column. Because a difference test is closed-form only for Pearson, Spearman, Kendall, and point-biserial (plus the six analytic-SE latent/ordinal methods in the *Between groups* structure – see [Method coverage](#method-coverage)), comparing any other coefficient (Cramér's V, η², a mutual-information measure, and so on) needs the bootstrap path – so under *Analytic* the card shows no statistic and a note prompting you to switch **Confidence intervals** to *Bias-corrected percentile bootstrap* to compare those cells. The same prompt covers **asymmetric coefficients** under the overlapping and non-overlapping structures, which have no closed-form difference test at all (the variance formula needs supplementary cross-correlations that fall on invalid same-role pairs).

Two failures get a message of their own instead of that prompt, because switching to bootstrap wouldn't rescue them: *one of the two correlations sits at ±1, where the Fisher-z transform is undefined*, and *too few complete cases in one of the groups for a difference test*. Both name the actual obstacle rather than inviting a setting change that would change nothing. A comparison that asks for the reverse direction of a cell the analysis couldn't compute is shown with its *could not be computed* tooltip too, rather than quietly vanishing from the table – the same failure now reads the same way whichever direction the enumeration reaches it from.

A comparison whose two cells were computed with **different effective methods** is never tested at all – possible under η², where a categorical × categorical pair automatically falls back to Cramér's V, so an η² cell (an r²-scale number) could otherwise be paired with a V cell (an r-scale one). The row suppresses Δr and shows a *not comparable* marker whose tooltip explains the mismatch.

### P-value adjustment for comparisons

The same global [adjustment method](./settings.md#multiple-comparison-adjustment) that applies to the matrix is applied separately to the family of diff-test p-values – the matrix's p-values and the diff card's p-values are adjusted **independently**, since they answer different families of questions. Inside the diff card, all comparisons of a single structure are treated as one family.

### Reporting comparisons

When writing up a difference test, report: the comparison structure, the test family (Williams T2 / Steiger Z / Fisher z / between-groups Wald z / bootstrap with B), the two coefficients, Δr **and its confidence interval** (Zou or bootstrap), the test statistic with df, the p-value (raw and adjusted if applicable), and the sample size(s). For omnibus tests, report χ², df, p, and which omnibus variant was used (Steiger pattern, Jennrich, Wald on Olkin–Siotani, or ACAT fallback).

## Display options

### Table format

- **Matrix** (default) – correlation matrix with variables on both axes
- **Long format** – flat table with one row per variable pair

### P-value display (matrix only)

- **Combined with correlation** (default) – each cell shows the coefficient with significance stars on one line and the p-value below it
- **Separate p-value table** – the matrix shows only coefficients, and separate p-value matrices appear below

### Hide redundant values

Enabled by default. When the matrix is symmetric (same variables on both axes), only the lower triangle is shown. Uncheck to see the full matrix. The checkbox is automatically hidden when an asymmetric method (Somers' D, Theil's U, Chatterjee's ξ) is selected – in those cases both triangles carry genuinely different values and neither is redundant.

### Append MI / Append entropies

Two checkboxes appear below the information-theoretic methods (NMI, AMI, coherence, Theil's U) when one of them is selected:

- **Append MI** – adds the mutual information (in nats, Miller-Madow corrected) to each cell or long-format row. Useful for reporting and for reconciling with other tools, since the normalized statistic alone hides the absolute information content. Note this is the Miller-Madow-corrected value that reconciles with the appended entropies, not the plug-in MI the [analytic χ² independence test](#p-value-method-it-methods-only) uses.
- **Append entropies** – adds H(row) and H(col), the marginal entropies of each variable. Makes it easy to see whether a low NMI reflects genuine independence or just a low-entropy variable with little information to share in the first place.

Both are off by default to keep the default view uncluttered.

### Discretization bins

The information-theoretic measures cannot read continuous data directly – they need a contingency table – so every continuous variable is first cut into equal-frequency bins. Two controls beside the IT options decide how fine that grid is:

- **Discretization bins** – the rule that sets the bin count. **Cube root of N (conservative)** is the default (`≈ ∛n`); **Rice rule** doubles it (`2·∛n`); **Sturges' rule** uses `log₂ N + 1`; and **Fixed count** hands the choice to you.
- **Bins per variable** – appears only under *Fixed count*, and takes the exact number of bins (2–50) each continuous variable is cut into.

Every rule is capped at the variable's number of distinct values, and a variable with few distinct values is coded level for level rather than binned – so the setting only ever moves genuinely continuous columns.

> **Finer is not better.** A finer grid resolves more structure, but it also spreads the same rows over more cells, and a sparse joint table inflates mutual information itself – the coefficient, not just its p-value. On the same pair, NMI measures **0.057** under the cube-root rule (a 6×6 grid), **0.117** under Rice (12×12) and **0.073** at a fixed 3 bins. None of those is more correct than the others; they are answers to different questions. Because the grid is part of the estimand, coefficients from runs at different bin settings are **not comparable**, and a caveat under the table names the rule whenever it is not the default. The [pre-flight advisory](#information-theoretic-methods-discretized-grid) and the bootstrap intervals both run on the same grid the coefficients do, so the interval always brackets the estimate it belongs to.

### P-value method (IT methods only)

A dropdown appears alongside the IT options when an information-theoretic method (NMI, AMI, coherence, Theil's U) is selected:

- **Analytic chi² (fast, tests independence)** – default. Uses the asymptotic result that `2n·MI ~ χ²((k_x−1)(k_y−1))` under independence. The test is correct for the *independence hypothesis* but its statistic is raw MI, not the normalized coefficient you see in the cell. Fast – single closed-form call per pair. Because it leans on a χ² approximation, its accuracy degrades when the discretized joint table has low expected cell counts (< 5), and permutation is the remedy. The expected-count check runs in **both** modes, not just this one – a sparse table inflates the mutual-information *coefficient* itself, so a coefficient-level caveat fires whichever mode you picked, with the analytic-p caveat added on top when you're on this route. For **adjusted mutual information** the analytic p is only ever approximate – unlike the other IT measures, AMI is not a monotone transform of MI at fixed margins – so a caveat notes this whenever AMI is run analytically; permutation is exact.
- **Permutation (slower, tests the reported coefficient)** – shuffles one variable many times and recomputes the chosen coefficient on each shuffle. The shuffle count is derived from your [significance level](./settings.md#significance-level) (at least `20/α` reps so the threshold can be resolved) with your [bootstrap-replications setting](./settings.md#bootstrap-replications) as a floor; when p-adjustment is active, the count is inflated by `√(number of pairs)` to maintain resolution after correction. The reported p-value is the fraction of shuffles whose coefficient reaches or exceeds the observed value (with the standard +1/+1 finite-sample correction). This directly tests "is the reported NMI/AMI/coherence/Theil's U larger than chance?", which is most defensible for AMI, where the chi² approximation is only loose.

This dropdown governs the information-theoretic coefficients only. Distance correlation and the contingency-table coefficients have their own p-value dropdowns – see the two sections below.

### P-value method (Pearson, rank and ordinal methods)

The same style of dropdown appears when **Pearson's r**, **Spearman's rho**, **Kendall's tau**, **Goodman & Kruskal's γ** or **Somers' D** is selected – and under **Mixed/auto (observed scores)**, which dispatches among them:

- **Analytic (fast, approximate under ties, non-normality or small samples)** – default. `cor.test`'s standard p-value for Pearson and the rank methods; an atanh Wald test on the tie-aware ASE for γ and Somers' D. Reliable for mostly-distinct, roughly normal data. With heavy ties the exact small-sample distributions don't apply and the asymptotic approximation the test falls back on degrades – exactly the situation the [tie-burden check](#rank--ordinal-methods-tie-burden) flags. Pearson's *t* test additionally leans on bivariate normality, so a failed [OLS diagnostic](#ols-diagnostics-pearson) is a second reason to switch. And for **γ** / **Somers' D** the analytic route has a hard blind spot: when the coefficient reaches ±1 – which γ does on any table with no discordant pairs – the ASE is exactly 0 and no p-value, standard error or interval can be reported at all.
- **Permutation (slower, exact independence test)** – shuffles one variable many times and recomputes the coefficient on each shuffle, reporting the two-sided fraction of shuffles that reach the observed magnitude (with the standard +1/+1 finite-sample correction). The shuffle count follows the same rules as the information-theoretic permutation option above. Because the null distribution is built from your own data, this is an exact test of independence however heavy the ties, however skewed the marginals and however small the sample – it is the remedy the tie-burden note recommends, the distribution-free answer when Pearson's normality assumption is rejected, and the only route to a p-value for a perfect γ or Somers' D. Kendall's τ costs O(n²) per shuffle, so selecting it warns you up front with the pair count, sample size and replication count before the run starts.

The dropdown **stays available when [control variables](#controlling-for-covariates-partial--semi-partial-correlation) are active**. A plain column shuffle has no partial-correlation meaning – it would break the pair's link to the controls along with the link to each other – so under covariates the permutation option switches to the **Freedman–Lane** scheme: the outcome is regressed on the controls (on the scale `ppcor` inverts – raw values for Pearson, ranks otherwise), only the *residuals* are shuffled, the fitted values are added back, and the coefficient is recomputed from that surrogate through the same `pcor.test` the point estimate uses. Holding the controls fixed and permuting only what is left of the association is what makes the null the conditional one – X ⊥ Y *given* Z – rather than full independence. In semi-partial mode the reference statistic stays the partial (`pcor`) estimate, matching the p the cell already displays. Because the surrogate is rebuilt rather than merely relabelled, the test is **approximate rather than exact**, and a caveat under the table says so whenever covariates and permutation are both active.

> **Ties get one extra step.** The Freedman–Lane surrogate (fitted values + shuffled residuals) is tie-free even when your data is not, and Kendall's τ<sub>b</sub> normalizes by the tie pattern – so the observed statistic would sit on a different scale from its own null distribution. DataSuite therefore maps the surrogate back onto the outcome's own multiset of values before taking the statistic. It matters: measured under the conditional null at n = 60, a partial Kendall on 4-level data rejects at **.348** analytically and **.104** under the plain surrogate, against **.052** with the remap. On untied data the remap is a monotone relabelling of a rank statistic's input – a no-op, reproducing the un-remapped p exactly.

### P-value method (distance correlation)

**Distance correlation** gets its own dropdown, and unlike the two above it defaults to the *slower* option:

- **Permutation (slower, correctly sized)** – default. Shuffles one variable `R` times and rebuilds the distance covariance on each shuffle (`energy::dcov.test`), so the reference distribution comes from your own data. Measured rejection rate at α = 0.05 on independent data: 0.045 – i.e. correctly sized.
- **Analytic t (fast, anti-conservative in the tail)** – the `energy::dcorT.test` *t*-approximation. It is asymptotic in the *dimension* of the data, not in `n`, so it over-rejects on the two-column pairs this module builds, and the inflation doesn't shrink as the sample grows. Measured rejection rate at α = 0.05: 0.089 / 0.060 / 0.060 at n = 20 / 50 / 200; at α = 0.01: 0.045 / 0.026 / 0.034 – three to four times nominal in exactly the tail that [p-value adjustment](./settings.md#multiple-comparison-adjustment) pushes results into.

The coefficient is identical in both modes; only the p-value changes. The permutation route costs `R` times more than the analytic one, though – it rebuilds the O(n²) distance matrices once per shuffle, and it does so inside compiled code that **cannot be interrupted** once a cell starts, so **Cancel** won't reach it. Rather than let a cell run for minutes, DataSuite budgets the work per cell in `replications × n²` units: above roughly 2 000 rows the shuffle count is traded down to fit (a caveat under the table reports the count actually used, and the p-value is correspondingly coarser), and above roughly 2 200 rows even the 199-shuffle resolution floor overruns the budget, so the cell reports *too many rows for an uninterruptible permutation test* and asks you to switch to the analytic route. Pick the analytic route when you need a fast screen and won't be reading its tail – or when your sample is large enough that permutation is refused.

### P-value method (contingency-table methods)

**Phi** and **Cramér's V** take their p-value from a χ² test on the pair's contingency table, and get a dropdown of their own:

- **Analytic χ² (fast, needs adequate expected counts)** – default. `chisq.test`'s asymptotic p-value. Reliable when the [expected cell counts](#contingency-table-methods-χ²-cell-counts) are adequate; unreliable when they aren't, which is exactly what the assumption card's *Adequate expected counts* verdict tells you.
- **Monte Carlo (slower, valid on sparse tables)** – simulates tables with the observed margins and reports the fraction reaching the observed statistic. Valid however sparse the table is, at the cost of the simulation (it uses your [permutation replication count](./settings.md#permutation-replications) and a pinned seed, so the result is reproducible).

The **coefficient is byte-identical** in either mode – only the p-value moves. This is the remedy the sparse-cells advisory points at: switch here rather than dropping φ / Cramér's V from your analysis.

### Confidence intervals

A dropdown controls whether each coefficient is reported with a confidence interval:

- **None** (default) – no CIs are computed.
- **Analytic (closed-form where supported)** – uses each method's natural closed-form CI: Fisher *z* transform for Pearson / Spearman (Bonett–Wright variance) / Kendall (data-dependent Brown–Benedetti τ<sub>b</sub> ASE for zero-order, delta-method-transformed onto the *z* scale; partials fall back to the Fieller–Hartley–Pearson constant) / point-biserial, and an **atanh-transformed (Fisher *z*) Wald interval with delta-method SE** for polychoric, polyserial, tetrachoric, biserial, Goodman & Kruskal's γ, and Somers' D – keeping the bounds inside the natural `[-1, 1]` range. Three of the unsigned measures also gain a proper closed-form interval, each built from the very statistic behind the coefficient: **η²** by inverting the noncentral *F* of its one-way ANOVA, **Cramér's V** by inverting the noncentral χ² of its contingency table onto the Bergsma-corrected scale (so the interval matches the bias-corrected coefficient and collapses toward 0 under independence), and **Blomqvist's β** from a Wilson score interval on the concordant-quadrant proportion (mapped back through β = 2p − 1, so the bounds stay inside `[-1, 1]` even at β = ±1). **Chatterjee's ξ** likewise gets a plain-scale (non-Fisher) Wald interval `ξ ± z·SE`, clamped to ξ's natural `[0, 1]` range (a low estimate with a wide SE floors its lower bound at 0 rather than reporting a nonsensical negative value), built on a **data-adaptive envelope standard error** – the larger of a consistent alternative-variance estimate (Lin & Han 2023) and the independence-null variance (Chatterjee 2021), so the interval is never narrower than it would be under independence, never degenerates on small samples, and widens when the data's dependence structure implies more variability than independence. Because ξ is a downward-biased finite-sample estimator, treat its analytic interval as a fast approximation and prefer **bootstrap** on small samples (the exact alternative) – measured over 400 replicates at *n* = 200, the analytic interval covers about **92%** against a nominal 95%, and a caveat under the table says so whenever a ξ cell shows one. The **φ coefficient** rides on the same noncentral-χ² inversion as Cramér's V: on a 2×2 table |φ| *is* the classical V, so the interval inverts the very χ² φ's p-value comes from – on the unshrunk denominator that matches the raw φ estimate – and is then re-signed to match the coefficient. That route sidesteps φ's awkward asymptotic standard error, which depends on the 2×2 marginal proportions rather than on φ alone, so a Fisher-*z* interval would disagree with the χ² test. Every remaining method has no defensible closed-form CI and is **bootstrap-only** – the information-theoretic family (NMI, AMI, coherence, Theil's U), Hoeffding's D, and distance correlation. Pick the bootstrap mode for any of these; under *Analytic* they show `–`, and the CI column is dropped entirely rather than rendered empty when *no* cell in the run produced an interval. A caveat under the table names the method and points at the bootstrap mode whenever **any** cell has no analytic route – including a mixed run where a supported method beside it fills the column, and the Spearman / Kendall **semi-partials**, whose partial-*r* Fisher SE assumes both sides conditioned and so lose the interval their zero-order form has. Under the bootstrap mode a parallel caveat covers cells whose resamples thinned below the 80 % acceptance gate, so a blank interval is never left unexplained. One further analytic interval carries a health warning of its own: **point-biserial**'s dichotomous margin breaks the binormality the Fisher-*z* interval assumes and its sampling variance depends on the group split (Tate 1954), so when the binary side is more lopsided than roughly 20/80 a caveat points at the bootstrap mode. A second warning guards the other end of the range the latent methods can reach: the atanh Wald standard error carries a `1/(1 − ρ²)` factor, so an association close to perfect back-transforms to bounds spanning almost the whole `[-1, 1]` range however precisely the coefficient itself was estimated – a perfectly associated 2×2 table gives ρ<sub>tet</sub> = 1.000 with an SE of 0.002 and prints `[-1.000, 1.000]` beside a *p* below 1e-50. That interval stays visible rather than being blanked, because the limit of the delta method is the honest thing to show; a caveat under the table says that an interval covering 0 beside a coefficient that strong is the degeneracy rather than weak evidence, and points at the bootstrap mode, which refits per resample and has no such boundary.
- **Bias-corrected percentile bootstrap (any method)** – resamples the data with replacement (using the [bootstrap settings](./settings.md#bootstrap-replications) for replications) and reads off empirical quantiles, corrected for median bias with a **bias-corrected (BC) percentile** adjustment. The correction recentres the interval when the bootstrap distribution is skewed – as it is for the bounded, non-negative measures whose values pile up near zero – and collapses back to the plain percentile interval when the distribution is symmetric, so the classical coefficients are unaffected. It is available for every method (including the partial / semi-partial Pearson / Spearman / Kendall variants), which makes it the universal fallback for the coefficients that have no closed-form CI.

The confidence level is read from your global [confidence-level setting](./settings.md#confidence-level). CIs appear as an extra column in the long table and as an extra line under the coefficient in matrix cells. When a [difference test](#testing-differences-between-correlations) is active, the same setting also adds per-coefficient and Δr intervals to the [comparison table](#reading-the-comparison-table). Methods without a supported CI for the chosen mode show `–`.

> **Which mode to pick?** Analytic is faster and produces narrower (more efficient) intervals when its assumptions hold. Bootstrap is more flexible but slower and a bit wider – useful when sample sizes are small, distributions are weird, or you simply don't trust the asymptotic approximation. For a quick check, analytic; for a final report on a small or non-normal dataset, bootstrap.

> **The replication count is part of the interval.** A percentile interval's endpoints are quantiles of the resampled distribution, so its resolution is roughly `1/B`. At the shipped default of 100 [bootstrap replications](./settings.md#bootstrap-replications), a nominal 95% interval measures about **91%** actual coverage; at 1000 it reaches about **93.5%**. The setting itself goes as low as 10, which no interval can survive, so this module floors it at **100** – a value below that is raised rather than published. Above the floor DataSuite does not silently override your setting, but it does disclose the gap: whenever bootstrap CIs are on and the replication setting is below 1000, a caveat under the table states the measured under-coverage and suggests raising it. Raise it before reporting an interval; the run is slower, but the number means what it says.

> **An interval that can't support its own quantiles isn't drawn.** Some resamples fail outright – a degenerate polychoric table, a rank partial that can't be inverted – and a 2.5% quantile taken over a handful of survivors is not a 95% bound. A cell renders its interval only when at least **80%** of the requested replications produced a usable value; below that it shows `–` rather than a number with no standing behind it.

> **Latent methods with many categories:** the analytic latent-correlation intervals (polychoric / polyserial / tetrachoric / biserial) use a two-step Wald SE that is conditional on the estimated category thresholds, so it runs **slightly narrow** as the number of categories grows – negligible for a 2×2 tetrachoric, drifting toward mild anti-conservatism for fine tables. When such a pair has a many-category variable, the cell adds an inline nudge to switch to **Bias-corrected percentile bootstrap**, which refits the estimate on every resample and so captures the threshold uncertainty exactly.

### Negligible-correlation margin (TOST)

By default, a correlation analysis can only tell you whether a relationship *exists* – a non-significant p-value never proves a correlation is absent (see the [note on interpretation](#interpretation)). The **Negligible-correlation margin** lets you make that positive claim. Enter a margin Δ – the largest coefficient you'd still consider practically zero – and DataSuite runs an equivalence test for every pair. Leave it empty (the default) to skip. **Every method in the catalogue is covered**, but the null being tested depends on whether the coefficient is signed, so the test comes in two shapes:

- **Signed coefficients – two-sided TOST.** Pearson, Spearman, Kendall, point-biserial, biserial, polyserial, polychoric, tetrachoric, Somers' D, Goodman & Kruskal's γ, Blomqvist's β and the φ coefficient pit H₀ |ρ| ≥ Δ against H₁ |ρ| < Δ: two one-sided tests on the Fisher-*z*–transformed coefficient against the bounds −Δ and +Δ, with the reported **p (Δ)** being the larger of the two. Most use a closed-form standard error; **Blomqvist's β** derives its from the concordant-quadrant proportion behind the coefficient (the same proportion its Wilson CI uses), and the **φ coefficient** – whose asymptotic SE depends on the 2×2 margins rather than on φ alone – uses a bootstrap *z*-scale standard error computed on demand whenever a margin is set, so φ gets a TOST p alongside its noncentral-χ² interval. Choosing the **bias-corrected percentile bootstrap** for [confidence intervals](#confidence-intervals) switches this whole family onto the resampled standard error: the interval and the equivalence verdict on one card then rest on the same sampling distribution, rather than on a bootstrap CI beside a Fisher-*z* p. A TOST at α corresponds to the **(1 − 2α)** interval lying inside ±Δ – *not* the (1 − α) interval printed beside it – so the caption under the table spells that level out.
- **Unsigned measures – one-sided.** η², Cramér's V, NMI, AMI, Rajski's coherence, Theil's U, Hoeffding's D, Chatterjee's ξ and distance correlation live on `[0, 1]`, where a lower test against −Δ is vacuous. They test the single null H₀ θ ≥ Δ against H₁ θ < Δ, and **p (Δ)** is that one test's p-value – which at α corresponds to the upper end of the **(1 − 2α)** interval falling below Δ, again named in the caption.

A small **p (Δ)** is positive evidence that the true coefficient falls inside the band, and is therefore negligible. Because a single table can mix the two shapes (a Mixed/Auto run, or an unsigned method that falls back to another), a caption under the results always states the margin actually used and which null each family was tested against – so **p (Δ)** is never left to be read as "some equivalence test at some margin".

> **Δ is a raw margin, not a rescaled one.** Whatever the method, Δ is compared against the coefficient exactly as the table prints it – no measure is mapped onto a common scale first. That is the honest reading of "the largest coefficient you would still consider practically zero", but it does mean one number marks out different amounts of association from cell to cell: Δ = 0.3 against Pearson's *r* is a moderate linear association, against ε² it is 30% of the variance explained, and against an information measure 30% of the shared-information ceiling. A caption states this whenever any cell carries a **p (Δ)**, and a second line warns you when one run's equivalence cells span more than one such scale, since then their verdicts are not comparable with each other even at the same margin. "Scale" here is exactly the [strength-band set](#interpretation) a coefficient reads on – the coefficients the module already treats as reading alike are one scale.

The unsigned family reaches its one-sided p by three different routes, picked per method so nothing is resampled that doesn't have to be:

- **η² and Cramér's V invert their own noncentral distribution** – the noncentral *F* of the one-way ANOVA and the noncentral χ² of the contingency table respectively. This is exact, needs no standard error and costs nothing extra, and it makes the TOST and the [analytic CI](#confidence-intervals) two views of one calculation: set Δ to the interval's upper bound and **p (Δ)** comes back at exactly your α.
- **Chatterjee's ξ** uses the same data-adaptive envelope standard error as its CI, on its plain `[0, 1]` scale.
- **The six with no closed-form SE at all** – NMI, AMI, coherence, Theil's U, Hoeffding's D and distance correlation – buy a per-cell **bootstrap standard error** on demand, the same way φ does. Setting a margin therefore triggers a resample for these six even when confidence intervals are off; that is deliberate, and it is the only extra cost.

> **Why a floor on p (Δ) built on a resampled SE.** A standard error estimated from `B` replicates carries a few percent of relative error, and a normal-approximation p is exquisitely sensitive to it – a 7% wobble in the SE can move the p by tens of orders of magnitude, so an unfloored value like `3e-246` says far more than the resample can support. DataSuite therefore floors **p (Δ)** at the resample's own resolution, `1/(B+1)` – 0.0099 at the default 100 replications, 0.002 at 500 – wherever the standard error behind it was resampled: those six measures, φ, the Spearman / Kendall semi-partials, and the whole signed family when you pick the bootstrap CI. A cell sitting exactly at the floor is the **strongest** equivalence claim the replication count supports, not a marginal result; a caveat under the table names the affected symbols. Raise the [bootstrap replications](./settings.md#bootstrap-replications) if you need a finer number. The routes with a genuine closed-form standard error – and η² / Cramér's V, which invert an exact distribution – are not floored.

- **Its own adjustment family** – because it asks the opposite question from the ordinary p-value, **p (Δ)** is [adjusted](./settings.md#multiple-comparison-adjustment) separately from the significance p-values, never pooled with them.

> **Choosing Δ:** set it to the smallest |r| that would be practically meaningful in your field, *before* looking at the results – picking a margin just wide enough to reach significance invalidates the test. A margin that's too wide makes negligibility easy to claim but unconvincing; too narrow demands very large samples. This is the correlation-scale analogue of the equivalence bound in [comparison analysis](./comparison-analysis.md#test-direction), where the same TOST logic is explained in more depth.

## Reading results

### Matrix format

Each cell shows:

- The correlation coefficient with the method's symbol (r, ρ, τ, β, φ, V, d<sub>yx</sub>, γ, η², ρ<sub>poly</sub>, ρ<sub>tet</sub>, ρ<sub>ps</sub>, NMI, AMI, C<sub>R</sub>, U, D<sub>H</sub>, ξ, dCor)
- Significance stars based on your [significance settings](./settings.md#significance-formatting)
- The p-value (formatted according to your [p-value settings](./settings.md#p-value-settings))
- Adjusted p-value, if [adjustment](./settings.md#multiple-comparison-adjustment) is enabled in addition mode
- **N** – the number of complete cases for the pair. Under [pairwise deletion](#missing-data) this varies from pair to pair, and both the p-value and the CI width depend on it
- **Test statistic** – the omnibus test behind an effect-size coefficient: η² shows its **F(df₁, df₂)**, while Cramér's V and the φ coefficient show their **χ²(df)** (including the η² → Cramér's V fallback rows), with the same significance stars as the coefficient – shown only for methods backed by a distinct omnibus test
- Confidence interval, if **Confidence intervals** is set to *Analytic* or *Bias-corrected percentile bootstrap* (and the method supports it)
- **SE** – the coefficient's closed-form Wald standard error, shown for the latent and ordinal methods that report one (polychoric, tetrachoric, polyserial, biserial, Goodman & Kruskal's γ, Somers' D). **Chatterjee's ξ** fills the same column with a different quantity: the data-adaptive *envelope* SE behind its interval and equivalence test, not the null-independence SE its p-value rests on – so dividing ξ by it will not reproduce ξ's printed p, and a caveat under the table says so
- Zero-order coefficient (with subscript ₀), if a partial / semi-partial run is active – the same correlation computed without the covariates
- The negligibility p-value **p (Δ)** (and **p (Δ, adj)** if adjustment is shown in addition mode), if a [**Negligible-correlation margin**](#negligible-correlation-margin-tost) is set and the method supports TOST – a small value is evidence the correlation is practically zero
- MI and/or entropies, if **Append MI** / **Append entropies** is enabled (IT methods only) – reported in **nats**, Miller-Madow-corrected, and describing the *discretized* variables rather than the raw ones, which a caveat under the table restates (an entropy slightly above `log(k)` for a k-bin variable is the correction, not an error)
- Diagonal cells show a dash (a variable's correlation with itself is always trivially 1)
- Error cells are highlighted in red and show the specific reason inline (e.g. "Insufficient data", "Not a 2×2 table", "Constant variable – no information"); the same message also appears in the cell's hover tooltip

For asymmetric methods (Somers' D, Theil's U, Chatterjee's ξ), a small *Direction: row → column* caption appears above the matrix – in long format it reads *Direction: variable 1 → variable 2* instead, naming that table's own columns. Each cell's value describes how well the **row variable predicts the column variable** – so the lower and upper triangles carry different numbers.

### Long format

Columns include:

- **Variable 1** and **Variable 2** – for asymmetric methods, the row reads as "Variable 1 → Variable 2" (Variable 1 is the predictor), and both orderings of each pair appear as separate rows
- **Method** – shown for Mixed/Auto, *and* whenever a per-row fallback occurred (e.g. η² automatically falls back to Cramér's V on categorical × categorical pairs); displays the symbol used for that pair, with the full method name in a tooltip
- **N** – the number of complete cases for the pair (pairwise-complete, after filters); varies from pair to pair under [pairwise deletion](#missing-data)
- **Coefficient** – the correlation value with significance stars
- **p-value** – and adjusted p-value if enabled in addition mode. The [adjustment](./settings.md#multiple-comparison-adjustment) family counts each *pair* once rather than each row: where both orderings carry the same p by construction – a **semi-partial** run (`pcor.test` is symmetric in the two variables) or **Theil's U** (its p comes from the symmetric mutual information) – the two rows share one adjusted value instead of doubling the family size. Genuinely direction-specific p's (Somers' D, Chatterjee's ξ) still count separately
- **Test statistic** – the omnibus test behind an effect-size coefficient: the correlation ratio η² shows its **F(df₁, df₂)**, while Cramér's V and the φ coefficient show their **χ²(df)** (this column also covers the η² → Cramér's V fallback rows). It carries the same significance stars as the coefficient, and appears only for methods backed by a distinct omnibus test
- **CI** – confidence interval column, if **Confidence intervals** is enabled
- **SE** – the standard error of the coefficient, shown for the latent and ordinal methods that report a closed-form Wald standard error (polychoric, tetrachoric, polyserial, biserial, Goodman & Kruskal's γ, Somers' D); complements the CI as a pooling-ready measure of precision. **Chatterjee's ξ** fills the same column with its data-adaptive *envelope* SE – the one behind its interval and equivalence test, not the null-independence SE its p-value uses – so ξ ÷ SE does not reproduce ξ's printed p; a caveat under the table says so
- **r₀ (ρ₀, τ₀, …)** – zero-order coefficient, if partials are active
- **p (Δ)** (and **p (Δ, adj)**) – the negligibility / TOST equivalence p-value, shown when a [**Negligible-correlation margin**](#negligible-correlation-margin-tost) is set and the method supports TOST
- **MI (nats), H(var₁) (nats), H(var₂) (nats)** – appended when the **Append MI** / **Append entropies** checkboxes are enabled for IT methods; the headers carry the unit and a caveat under the table names the rest of the basis (Miller-Madow correction, computed on the *discretized* variables)
- **Interpretation** – if the [interpretation setting](./settings.md#significance-formatting) is turned on. For failed pairs, this column shows the specific reason for the error instead.

### Interpretation

When enabled, each correlation receives a plain-language description combining:

- Significance – "Significant" or "Non-significant"
- Strength – negligible (< 0.1), very weak (0.1–0.3), weak (0.3–0.5), moderate (0.5–0.7), strong (0.7–0.9), or very strong (≥ 0.9)
- Direction – positive or negative (signed methods only; unsigned methods omit this)

For example: "Significant moderate positive correlation" (Pearson) or "Significant strong association" (η², NMI, Chatterjee's ξ, and other unsigned measures). Strength thresholds are configurable – see [settings](./settings.md) for the correlation, information-metric, η², Cramér's V, Hoeffding's D, and Chatterjee's ξ band options (η², Cramér's V, Hoeffding's D, and Chatterjee's ξ each get their own scale-appropriate defaults). The **φ coefficient** is labelled on the **Cramér's V** bands rather than the Pearson-*r* ones: on a 2×2 table |φ| *is* Cohen's *w* (`√(χ²/n)`), the quantity V generalizes, so the χ²-family cutoffs are the calibrated ones for it – the same 2×2 association then reads the same strength whichever of the two you pick, and φ keeps its signed *positive* / *negative* direction label.

> **Concordance measures run larger.** Goodman & Kruskal's γ and Somers' D are labelled with the same Pearson-*r* thresholds above, but they tend to produce larger magnitudes than *r* for the same underlying association – so a γ of 0.6 doesn't carry the same weight as a Pearson *r* of 0.6. A note under the matrix flags this; treat the strength label as approximate for these measures.

> **Kendall's τ and Blomqvist's β are labelled on the *r* scale.** For the same association both tend to come out *below* Pearson's *r* (under bivariate normality each follows ≈ (2/π)·arcsin *r* – so *r* = 0.5 corresponds to τ ≈ 0.33). To keep the strength label comparable to the other coefficients, DataSuite converts each back to its *r*-equivalent (`sin(π·stat/2)`) before applying the thresholds, so the label reports the same association strength Pearson's *r* would. Unlike γ and Somers' D above, no "runs smaller" note appears under the matrix; the labels already sit on the *r* scale.

> **Rajski's coherence is rescaled onto the NMI band scale.** The information-metric bands are written for NMI (`2·MI/(H(X)+H(Y))`), while coherence is `MI/H(X,Y)` – exactly `NMI/(2 − NMI)`, so it sits a full band low across the range (an NMI of 0.50 is a coherence of 0.333). Before applying the thresholds, DataSuite maps coherence back with the inverse `2C/(1+C)`, the same device it uses for Kendall's τ and Blomqvist's β, so the label reports the same association strength as the other information measures. Theil's U and AMI need no such map – both track NMI with no fixed offset. The *displayed coefficient* is untouched; only the strength label is computed on the mapped value.

> **η² is bias-corrected too.** The reported value is ε² = `1 − (1 − η²)·(n − 1)/(n − k)`, not the raw sum-of-squares ratio – see [above](#beyond-classical-correlations). The Cohen bands (.01 / .06 / .14) are population variance-explained cutoffs, so ε² is the quantity they were written for; the raw ratio would read a band or two high with many groups and a modest sample.

> **Cramér's V is bias-corrected.** DataSuite reports the **Bergsma (2013)** bias-corrected V, not the classical formula. The classical estimator is inflated at small *n* and large tables – it can show a positive value even when the two variables are independent – whereas the corrected version converges to it for non-tiny samples and can read exactly 0 under independence. The strength labels still use Cohen's thresholds, which are calibrated for large samples, so treat them as approximate for small samples or large tables.

> **Why "non-significant" doesn't mean "no relationship":** a non-significant result means there isn't enough evidence to conclude a relationship exists *in the population* – not that the variables are definitely unrelated. With small samples, even moderate correlations can be non-significant simply because there isn't enough data. With very large samples, even tiny correlations can be significant while being practically meaningless. Always consider the coefficient size alongside the p-value.

> **Proving a correlation is negligible:** if you actually want to claim two variables are *practically unrelated* – not merely "not significant" – use the [**Negligible-correlation margin**](#negligible-correlation-margin-tost) to run a formal equivalence test. Its **p (Δ)** turns "we found no significant correlation" into the defensible "the correlation is significantly within ±Δ of zero."

## P-value adjustment

Correlation matrices involve many simultaneous tests – a 10-variable matrix produces 45 unique pairs. Without adjustment, some results will appear significant by chance alone.

If no [adjustment method](./settings.md#multiple-comparison-adjustment) is selected, a warning appears recommending you consider one.

## Missing data

Missing values are handled by the global [missing data setting](./settings.md#missing-data):

- **Pairwise** (default) – each pair uses all cases where both variables have values
- **Listwise** – only cases complete across all selected variables are used
- **Imputation** – missing values are replaced with substitutes (mean, median, mode, or a constant) before analysis

> **Pairwise vs. listwise:** pairwise keeps more data but can produce correlation matrices that aren't internally consistent (e.g. variable A correlates with B and B with C, but the A–C correlation seems off because different subsets of cases were used). Listwise avoids this but may discard a lot of data if missingness is spread across many variables.

> **A note on imputation:** replacing missing values can artificially reduce variability, which tends to pull correlations toward zero. Mean and median imputation are the most prone to this. If you have a lot of missing data, consider whether pairwise deletion might be more appropriate for correlation analysis.

## Visualizations

All visualizations can be resized by dragging the handle in the bottom-right corner, and each has its own SVG / PNG / JPG export buttons that appear beside it on hover. To save every figure at once instead, use the bulk export action – see [resizing and exporting charts](./getting-started.md#resizing-and-exporting-charts) – which captures every plot on the page in one step.

> **Mixed/Auto coloring.** A Mixed/Auto run can put signed and unsigned coefficients in the same picture, and they don't share a color scale. Pairs computed by a signed method keep the blue-to-red diverging scale; pairs computed by an unsigned method (Cramér's V, η², the information-theoretic family, …) are colored on a separate green 0-to-max sequential ramp, so an unsigned 0.8 can't be misread as "strongly positive". The legend shows both strips – titled **Correlation:** and **Association:** so each one says what it encodes – and tooltips name each pair's actual method by its symbol. In the correlogram, unsigned cells never tilt left – they have no negative direction to encode.

> **Unsigned scales are normalized per method.** For unsigned methods (in a pure unsigned run or the unsigned cells of a Mixed/Auto one), both **color and glyph size** – ellipse eccentricity, edge thickness, force-link distance – are scaled to the strongest coefficient *that method* contributed, not to the theoretical 1. Unsigned statistics live in compressed ranges in practice (a dCor matrix rarely reaches 0.5), so absolute scaling would render most matrices as uniformly pale, fat glyphs; normalizing keeps within-matrix ranking readable. The normalization is **per method**, not per run, because unsigned measures don't share a scale: an η² of 0.30 is a very large effect and a Cramér's V of 0.30 a moderate one, so one shared ramp would make identical colour mean two different things. A run carrying more than one unsigned method – an η² matrix falling back to Cramér's V on its categorical pairs, or any Mixed/Auto run – therefore draws **one legend strip per method**, each titled with that method's symbol (`Association (η²):`, `Association (V):`), with the size-coupling caption emitted once below the last strip. Compare glyphs *within* a strip, and read the strip's printed top endpoint before comparing across strips or across runs. Signed methods are unaffected: their size and color encode the absolute |r| on the fixed −1…+1 scale.

> **A method with only one cell falls back to the absolute scale.** A single value *is* its own maximum, so normalizing it would paint it at full colour with a fully saturated glyph no matter how small it is. When a method contributes just one plottable cell, its strip runs `0…1` instead, so a lone Cramér's V of 0.40 reads as 0.40 rather than as 1. The legend caption names the strip's printed top rather than "the observed maximum", since for a singleton strip those are no longer the same thing.

### Edge bundling

A circular network diagram – the checkbox is labelled **Correlation network (edge bundling)** and the output card is titled *Correlation network*. Variables are arranged as labeled nodes around a circle, ordered by correlation similarity so strongly correlated variables sit closer together. Curved edges connect pairs with statistically significant correlations.

- **Edge color** encodes direction and strength – blue for positive, red for negative, gray for near-zero. A titled color legend (**Correlation:** / **Association:**) is drawn below the chart.
- **Edge thickness** reflects the absolute strength of the correlation
- **Negative edges are dashed** as well as red, so the sign survives a greyscale print or a colour-vision difference
- **Hover** over an edge to highlight it
- **Zoom** with the mouse wheel or the +/−/reset buttons in the top-right corner. Resizing the chart resets the zoom, so the redrawn graph always starts from a view that fits its new frame

### Force-directed graph

An interactive network where positively correlated variables attract each other and negatively correlated ones repel. Nodes are pill-shaped with variable labels inside. Edges are colored and sized by correlation strength and sign.

- **Drag** a node to reposition it – it stays fixed in place (shown with a blue dashed border)
- **Click** a fixed node to release it back into the simulation
- **Zoom** and **resize** work the same as edge bundling
- A **gradient color-key legend** for the edge color scale is drawn below the graph and is captured by image exports

- **Negative edges are dashed** and red here too, matching the edge-bundling convention

**Asymmetric runs get a directed graph.** When an asymmetric method (Somers' D, Theil's U, Chatterjee's ξ) is selected, or a semi-partial correlation is run – residualizing only one side of each pair makes the run directional – the force graph keeps **both directions of every pair** and draws them as gently bowed arcs with **arrowheads**, rather than collapsing them into one undirected line. The bow is what keeps the two apart: A → B and B → A sit on the same geometry and generally carry *different* values, so a straight line would hide one behind the other. Each arrowhead is pulled back to the edge of the target node's pill so the label doesn't bury it, and the tooltip spells the direction out (`X → Y`). A symmetric run draws no arrowheads, so the directed rendering never appears where it would be meaningless.

**Edge bundling stays unavailable for directional measures**, and now says so rather than vanishing: its checkbox is disabled with a visible note explaining that its arcs run between two positions on a circle and read the same in either direction, and pointing at the force-directed graph for direction. Directing it would need an arc convention *and* a second layout pass, and reads poorly at any density.

> **Which links are drawn.** Both graphs start from the significant pairs only, and both carry a **Show non-significant links (faded)** checkbox. A note under each graph states exactly what the current setting means – *Only pairs with p ≤ 0.050 are drawn; the rest are omitted*, or *Pairs with p > 0.050 are drawn faded* – and names the **adjusted** p when [p-value adjustment](./settings.md#multiple-comparison-adjustment) is active, since that is the threshold actually applied. Ticking the box rebuilds the *layout* rather than restyling it: node ordering, spacing and the forces all derive from the visible link set, so the picture with the faded links shown is the honest layout for that set, not the significant-only layout with extra lines on top. The **intensity** scales are deliberately left out of that rebuild: edge colour and thickness are normalized against every computable pair in the matrix, significant or not, so a link keeps the same colour and the same width in both states and the legend prints the full-matrix maximum. Normalizing against the visible set instead would paint the strongest *significant* link at full strength even when a stronger non-significant one exists – the two come apart as soon as pairwise deletion gives pairs different sample sizes, since only at a common n is the most significant link also the largest – and toggling the box would silently repaint every edge that stayed. A faded link keeps its own **sign dash** – the dash channel carries sign alone, so a non-significant *negative* pair still reads as negative – and on a directed run its arrowhead fades with the line instead of staying at full strength. One useful side effect – a run with *no* significant pairs now still draws the graph and its toggle, so you can switch the faded links on and see what's there instead of getting an empty card; when there is nothing plottable at all (every pair errored, say) the empty-state message says so rather than blaming the significance filter.

> **Edge bundling vs. force-directed:** the edge bundling layout is better for getting an overview of correlation structure – you can quickly see clusters of related variables. The force-directed graph is better for exploring specific relationships interactively, since you can pull nodes apart and rearrange the layout.

### Correlogram

A matrix of oriented ellipses – one per variable pair. The ellipse shape and orientation encode the correlation:

- **Positive correlations** tilt right (/) – **negative** tilt left (\)
- **Eccentricity** encodes absolute strength – a circle at r = 0, a thin line at r = ±1. For unsigned methods, eccentricity and color are normalized to the run's observed maximum (see the note [above](#visualizations))
- **Fill color** matches the correlation color scale (blue to red), with a gradient color-key legend below the matrix that is captured by image exports
- **Non-significant correlations** are dimmed with dashed borders
- A **key under the matrix** names the α behind that dimming and states what the tilt encodes – colour has its legend, and these two channels would otherwise go unlabelled
- The full matrix is shown (no diagonal)
- **Directional runs** carry the same *Direction: row → column* caption as the matrix, and each cell tooltip names its pair with an arrow (`A → B`) instead of the symmetric `A × B` – both triangles hold different numbers, so the tooltip has to say which of the two you are reading

If scatterplots are also enabled, clicking a cell scrolls to the corresponding scatterplot.

### Scatterplots

One scatter plot per variable pair, each in its own subsection. Each plot shows:

- Scatter points for the raw data
- A reference line whose shape depends on the chosen method:
	- **Pearson** and **point-biserial** – OLS regression line. A confidence band is drawn at your global confidence level when **Confidence intervals** is set to *Analytic* (closed-form conditional-mean band on the fitted line) or *Bias-corrected percentile bootstrap* (envelope of OLS lines refit on bootstrap resamples); no band is drawn when CIs are off. A **dashed LOWESS smoother** is overlaid beside the line whenever the pair has at least five *distinct* x values – curvature is the one thing a straight fit cannot report, and it is precisely what *r* misses. Below five distinct x values (a dichotomous predictor, say) the smoother would only retrace the line, so it is suppressed.
	- **Biserial** – the same straight OLS mean-difference line as point-biserial. As a normal-latent rescaling it has no valid analytic conditional-mean band, so only the *Bias-corrected percentile bootstrap* band is drawn; under *Analytic* the line shows with no band.
	- **Spearman**, **Kendall**, **polychoric**, **polyserial**, **Somers' D**, **Goodman & Kruskal's γ**, **Hoeffding's D**, **Chatterjee's ξ**, **distance correlation** – LOWESS smoother (no parametric line; reveals monotone or nonlinear structure)
	- **Blomqvist's β** – median crosshairs (vertical at median X, horizontal at median Y), since the coefficient itself is defined by sign agreement around those medians
	- **Tetrachoric** – no reference line. Both variables are binary, so the plot is a 2×2 grid of 0/1 points where a fitted line or smoother would be meaningless; the points still show the joint 0/1 distribution the coefficient is estimated from
	- **η²** – a horizontal tick at each level of the grouping side, drawn at that level's mean of the continuous side and joined by a dashed profile line. The spread of those level means *is* the correlation ratio, and the two-group case is exactly the point-biserial line above. It only appears when the grouping side is numeric-coded; a string-valued one cannot be plotted at all (below)
	- **Phi**, **Cramér's V**, **NMI**, **AMI**, **coherence**, **Theil's U** – no overlay; the pair is categorical and a continuous fit would mislead. When a pair can't be plotted at all (string-valued categories on either side), the subsection states *Scatterplot is not applicable to categorical data* instead of drawing an empty plot
- The coefficient with the method's symbol (r, ρ, τ, …), the p-value, and the pair's *n* in the corner – adjusted p-values follow the same [p-value adjustment display](./settings.md#multiple-comparison-adjustment) rule as the matrix and long-format tables. The *n* is the analysis sample for the pair (pairwise-complete, after filters), so it matches the table rather than a raw point count
- Plot range is padded by one tick interval so edge points aren't clipped
- Each pair sits in its own subsection titled with the two variable names – `A – B`, or `A → B` in a [directional run](#directional-asymmetric-methods), where both orderings appear as separate plots. That title is also the plot's accessible name, so a screen reader announces which pair it is on

> **What the LOWESS smoother is.** It fits a short straight line through the points nearest each x – weighted so the closest ones count most – and strings those local fits into a curve. Each window holds two thirds of the sample, and three robustness passes down-weight whatever the curve has already left far behind, so one or two stray points cannot drag it. It assumes no shape, which is exactly why it can show curvature a straight fit hides; it estimates nothing the tables report, so read it as a shape check rather than as a result.

> **Point-biserial and biserial put the dichotomy on X.** Regardless of which list you picked the variables from, these two plots put the binary variable on the horizontal axis and the continuous one on the vertical. With the binary on Y the OLS overlay would be a linear-probability line whose confidence band assumes homoscedastic normal residuals a 0/1 response cannot produce; with it on X, the identical fit is the two-group means line whose assumptions the [mean-comparison checks](#mean-comparison-methods-point-biserial-and-η²) already test. Polyserial keeps the continuous side on X, where its smoother needs the predictor.

> **A full Spearman partial draws a line, not a smoother.** Under *Partial (residualize both)*, both axes are rank residuals and the reported partial ρ is exactly the Pearson *r* of those two series – so the OLS line is the fit the reported number names, and that is what is drawn, with a confidence band under either CI mode. The analytic band there uses the same `df = n − 2 − k` that the cell's own p-value is computed against, so it is the band that inference already assumes. A LOWESS smoother there would depict no reported quantity. **Semi-partial** Spearman leaves one axis raw, where the smoother is still the right overlay, so it keeps one.

#### Grouped scatterplots (between groups / equality across groups)

When the active comparison structure is **Between groups** or the across-groups omnibus, the scatterplot card switches to a **grouped** layout – one subplot per variable pair, but with points and overlay lines colored by group. The single-sample pattern-equality omnibus has no comparison-aware scatter – its honest visualization is the correlogram.

- **Points and overlay** are drawn separately for each group, with the same method-specific overlay choice (OLS line / LOWESS smoother / median crosshairs / no overlay) applied within each series.
- **Colors** come from the project-wide Tableau-10 palette, in the same order the groups appear in the comparison table.
- **Per-group r and n** appear in the top-right legend, with each entry colored to match its series – so the legend doubles as the color key.
- **No confidence bands** are drawn in grouped mode – overlapping K bands is visually unreadable. Use the comparison table for the formal Δr statistic and its CI.
- **Residualization** for Pearson/Spearman partials runs *within each group*, so each group's residual scatter reflects its own conditional relationship (not a pooled residualization that would smear group differences).
- **Edge bundling, force-directed graph, and correlogram** are all skipped in grouped diff mode – they're inherently single-matrix displays and don't have a meaningful per-group rendering.

#### Anchor-overlay scatterplots (within row / within column / against reference)

When the active comparison structure is **Within each row variable**, **Within each column variable**, or **Against a reference cell**, the scatterplot card switches to an **anchor-overlay** layout – one subplot per shared anchor variable, with the comparator variables drawn as overlaid colored series on a shared X axis.

- **X axis** is the anchor (row variable in *within row*, column variable in *within column*, the shared variable in *against reference* when the reference and comparator share one). Each comparator variable contributes its own colored series with its own fit line and its own *r* and *n* in the legend.
- **Reference structure** only overlays pairs that share a variable with the reference cell. Non-overlapping comparators (no shared variable) have no honest shared-axis rendering and are omitted from the card; their Δr is still tested via Steiger Z and reported in the comparison table.
- **Residualization** for Pearson/Spearman partials runs once over the full sample (no group dimension), and each comparator series is residualized on the same covariates. In **semi-partial** mode only one side of each pair is residualized, so the panel commits to a single orientation – taken from its first comparator cell – and applies it consistently: either the anchor is raw on X with residualized comparators, or the anchor is residualized on X with raw comparators. The X axis label says which. (A per-series decision would put residual and raw data on one shared axis, which would be meaningless.)
- The **same overlay choice** as the single-mode scatter is used per series (OLS line / LOWESS smoother / median crosshairs / no overlay), so what you see matches the comparison test's underlying coefficient.

## Reporting checklist

Key things to include when writing up correlation results:

**Method:**
- Correlation method used (Pearson, Spearman, etc.) and why
- For partial / semi-partial analyses: the covariates controlled for, and whether you used partial or semi-partial (and on which side)
- For difference tests: the comparison family (pairwise vs. joint) and structure (within row / within column / against reference / between groups / pattern equality / equality across groups), the test used (Williams T2, Steiger Z, Dunn & Clark z at the back-transformed average, Fisher z, Jennrich χ², or bias-corrected percentile bootstrap with B replications) – for a dependent structure, name the [**Dependent test** variant](#dependent-test-variant) you left selected, since it changes the statistic – and – for *between groups* / *equality across groups* – the grouping variable. For *against a reference cell*, state whether the reference was pre-specified or picked from the data; for *pattern equality*, name the pattern (compound symmetry / equal within each row / equal within each column)
- For a non-default p-value route: that distance correlation used the permutation (or analytic) p, that φ / Cramér's V used the Monte-Carlo (or analytic) χ², and – where covariates were active alongside a permutation p – that the permutation followed the Freedman–Lane scheme and is therefore approximate
- For the information-theoretic measures: the [discretization rule and bin count](#discretization-bins), since the coefficients are only comparable within one grid
- How missing data were handled (pairwise or listwise deletion)
- P-value adjustment method, if any
- CI method (analytic / bootstrap) and confidence level, if reporting intervals
- For a negligibility test: that you used TOST, the margin Δ, and that Δ was chosen a priori
- Sample size

**Results:**
- The correlation coefficient with its symbol (r, ρ, τ, etc.)
- Confidence interval, if computed
- For partial analyses: the zero-order coefficient alongside, so readers can see the effect of controlling
- P-value (exact or inequality)
- For a negligibility test: the equivalence p-value **p (Δ)** (raw and adjusted if both are shown), the margin Δ used, and whether the test was two-sided (signed coefficient) or one-sided (unsigned measure). If **p (Δ)** sits at the `1/(B+1)` floor, report it as "≤ 1/(B+1) at B replications" rather than as an exact value
- Sample size per pair (if pairwise deletion was used and N varies)
- Effect size interpretation, if relevant
- For matrix output: whether the full matrix or selected pairs are reported
- For difference tests: both r's (or, for omnibus, the test alone), Δr and its confidence interval, the test statistic with df, and the p-value – adjusted and raw if both are shown

## Reproducibility

Every analysis prints the underlying R code to the [R console](./r-console.md) – you can inspect, copy, or re-run the exact commands. Correlation analysis uses base R (`cor.test`) for the classical methods, plus `polycor` (polychoric, polyserial, and tetrachoric), `infotheo` (mutual information and entropy with Miller-Madow correction), `aricode` (adjusted mutual information), `energy` (distance correlation – `dcov.test` for the default permutation p-value, `dcorT.test` for the analytic one), `Hmisc` (Hoeffding's D), `XICOR` (Chatterjee's ξ with ties-aware p-value), and `ppcor` (partial and semi-partial correlation for Pearson, Spearman, and Kendall – the semi-partial p-value taken from `pcor.test`, which tests the same null). Blomqvist's β, the correlation ratio ε², the signed φ coefficient, and Goodman & Kruskal's γ / Somers' D (with contingency-table ASE) are implemented directly in base R, as is the simulated χ² route for φ / Cramér's V. The negligibility-margin (TOST) p-values are likewise computed directly in base R: on the Fisher-*z* scale for the signed coefficients – from each method's closed-form standard error, or a proportion-delta SE for Blomqvist's β and an on-demand bootstrap SE for the φ coefficient – and on the plain scale for the unsigned measures, by inverting the noncentral *F* / χ² for η² and Cramér's V, from the envelope SE for Chatterjee's ξ, and from an on-demand bootstrap SE for the remaining six. Citations for any package your analysis actually uses appear automatically at the top of the output section.

The **correlation-comparison tests** are the exception to this R-everywhere rule: the pairwise difference tests (Fisher *z*, Williams' T2, Steiger Z), the omnibus tests (Jennrich χ², the Wald χ² on Olkin–Siotani covariance, the Steiger pattern-equality χ² and its bootstrap-calibrated Wald counterpart, and the Cauchy combination fallback), and the Δr confidence intervals (Zou's 2007 MOVER on the analytic path) run **in the browser**, not in R. They post-process the coefficients R returns – and, on the bootstrap path, an R-resampled correlation matrix – so they don't appear in the R console even though the inputs they consume do.

## Common pitfalls

**Correlation is not causation.** A strong correlation between ice cream sales and drowning rates doesn't mean ice cream causes drowning – both increase in summer. Correlation measures *association*, not causal direction. Establishing causation requires proper experimental design.

**Pearson's r only captures linear relationships.** Two variables can have a strong curvilinear relationship and still show r ≈ 0. Spearman's rho and Kendall's tau only help if the relationship is *monotonic* (consistently increasing or decreasing, even if non-linearly – e.g. exponential growth). They won't rescue you from a U-shaped or inverted-U pattern, which reverses direction and will produce near-zero coefficients with any of these methods. If you suspect a non-linear pattern, always visualize your data first – check the [distribution plots](./distribution-analysis.md#distribution-plots) or a scatter plot – before choosing a correlation method.

**Large matrices require care, not avoidance.** Running a 30×30 correlation matrix produces 435 tests – without correction, some will appear significant by chance. Always apply a multiple comparison correction when running a full matrix. The more important question is whether your analysis is hypothesis-driven or exploratory: if you're selecting "interesting" pairs after seeing the results, that's exploratory regardless of matrix size, and should be reported as such. If all pairs were theoretically motivated upfront and a correction was applied, a large matrix can support confirmatory claims.

**Outliers can dominate Pearson's r.** A single extreme point can inflate or deflate a Pearson correlation dramatically. If your data has outliers, Spearman's rho (which uses ranks) or Blomqvist's β (which uses only medians) is much more robust. Always visualize your data before trusting a single number.

**Unsigned measures aren't comparable to signed ones.** A Pearson *r* of 0.5 and an NMI of 0.5 mean very different things. Pearson's 0.5 is a moderate linear association; NMI's 0.5 says half of the combined variable entropy is shared – a much stronger statement. Don't treat the two scales as interchangeable when you're comparing across methods, and don't expect a signed method and an unsigned method to produce similar numbers on the same pair.

**Asymmetric measures need both directions.** When you use Somers' D, Theil's U, or Chatterjee's ξ, the value of "A → B" is generally **not** the same as "B → A". The upper and lower triangles of the matrix are both populated and both meaningful. If you're reporting a single number for an asymmetric measure, always specify which direction – "U(Y | X) = 0.42", not just "U = 0.42".

**Correlating two time-ordered series spuriously inflates *r*.** Two trending or seasonal series can show a near-perfect Pearson correlation just because they share the trend or cycle, not because they're related at any given moment. The classic textbook example is "US per-capita cheese consumption vs. deaths from bedsheet entanglement". Detrend and de-seasonalise first – the [Time series analysis](./time-series-analysis.md) module's exploration view shows the decomposition components you can correlate instead – or compute the cross-correlation of the differenced series.
