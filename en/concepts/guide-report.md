# Writing it up

The cards are on the screen; the last step is turning them into a paragraph a reader can check. This page says which numbers go into it, in what order, and what has to be said about how they were made – walked through on the four café findings of [Choosing an analysis](./guide-analysis.md). The rule underneath is the same for a café owner's memo and a journal article: a report gives the reader enough to judge the finding and enough to reproduce it, and nothing that is only there because the app printed it.

## Two paragraphs

A quantitative report has two parts that answer two different questions. The *Method* says what was done – the data, the fixes, the design, the test and the settings – so that someone with the same file could get the same card. The *Results* says what was found – the estimate, its uncertainty, the test and its verdict – so that the reader can judge it without seeing the card. Every module's manual ends with a [reporting checklist](../comparison-analysis.md#reporting-checklist) in exactly those two halves; the walk below is the checklist filled in.

### Method

The Method paragraph has five things in it, and the first three are the same whatever the analysis.

*The data.* Where it came from, how many cases, what the variables were and how they were measured: "200 loyalty-card members of a two-branch café, surveyed by e-mail eight weeks after a menu change; per-visit spend and visit counts for the eight weeks before and after from the till; six 1–5 agreement items, a 0–10 satisfaction score, a yes/no recommendation and the wait at the last visit from the survey." A reader who does not know what `overall` is cannot judge anything said about it, so define the variables in words, not by column name.

*What was done to it.* Every step of [the codebook page](./guide-data.md#three-things-to-fix-first) is a sentence here, because each one changed a number: "Branch was recorded as 1 and 2 and recoded to its names. Age was entered as 999 for five members who declined, and those cells were treated as missing. One member's after-window spend was a single catering order; that cell was excluded from the spend analyses (n = 199), the member's other data retained." State the rule, not just the outcome – *an after-window average above 50 is not a per-visit spend* – so that the reader can disagree with it. Then how missing values were handled: "analyses use the cases complete on their own variables (pairwise deletion), so n varies by analysis and is reported with each result." The [outliers and missing data](./outliers-missing-data.md#reporting) page has the full list of what to disclose.

*The design and the roles.* Which cases were compared and how they relate: "Branches were compared as independent groups; before-and-after measures were compared within members, each member serving as their own control." The reader needs the [design](./guide-analysis.md#how-are-the-cases-related) to know which test is appropriate, and it is the sentence most often left out.

*The tests and the settings.* The test by name, the direction, and the app-wide settings that shaped the p: "Welch's t-test for the branch comparisons, the paired t-test for the before–after change, Pearson's r for the correlations; all tests two-tailed at α = 0.05, with confidence intervals at 95%. The six survey items were compared between branches as one family, with Holm adjustment." A setting left at its default still goes in when it affects the numbers; the [settings](../settings.md) page marks the ones that do.

*The tools.* The software and the packages behind the tests, which the app's [R console](../r-console.md) and each manual's Reproducibility section name, and the citation the app collects in its citation card. One sentence: "Analyses were run in DataSuite 2, which computes its statistics with R (the packages are listed in the appendix)."

### Results

A result is one sentence per finding, and the sentence has a fixed skeleton: *the estimate in the outcome's own units, its confidence interval, the test with its statistic and degrees of freedom, the exact p, the effect size, the sample size.* In that order, because that is the order of importance – the reader wants to know how big first, how sure second, and the test statistic is the receipt. The [hypothesis testing](./hypothesis-testing.md#reporting-a-test), [effect sizes](./effect-sizes.md#reporting-an-effect-size) and [confidence intervals](./confidence-intervals.md#reporting-an-interval) pages each say what their part of the skeleton needs; here the four café findings assembled from them.

*A difference between groups.* "Station members spent more per visit than Riverside members before the menu change: 9.52 (SD 4.05) against 7.91 (SD 2.97), a difference of 1.61 (95% CI 0.62 to 2.60), Welch's t(179.5) = −3.20, p = 0.002, d = −0.45, n = 99 and 101." Everything the card showed is here, and the direction has been put into words – *Station more than Riverside* – so that no reader has to work out which group the app subtracted from which; the statistic and d keep the sign the card printed, which follows the app's group order. The group SDs are there because the reader can rebuild d from them; the group intervals are not, because the interval on the difference is the one that answers the question. A non-significant difference is reported in the same shape: "The branches did not differ in whether members would recommend the café – 72% at Riverside, 63% at Station, χ²(1) = 1.71, p = 0.19, V = 0.10 (95% CI 0 to 0.24), n = 200 – the interval on V allows anything from no association to a modest one." *Did not differ* means the test found no evidence; write "no significant difference" rather than "no difference" and let the interval say how much room is left.

*A change within the same cases.* "Spend per visit rose after the change by 1.09 on average (SD of the change 2.86, 95% CI 0.69 to 1.49), paired t(198) = 5.37, p < 0.001, dz = 0.38, n = 199. Visits per member rose by 0.64 (95% CI −0.003 to 1.28), t(199) = 1.96, p = 0.051, dz = 0.14, n = 200." The second sentence is the one to get right: it reports the boundary result exactly and lets the interval show that the data are compatible with no change and with a rise of a visit and a quarter. What it does not do is say "visits did not change" or drop the finding – a p of 0.051 rounds to nothing only in a report that is hiding it. The [boundary](./hypothesis-testing.md#a-result-on-the-boundary) section says why.

*A relationship.* "Overall satisfaction correlated with the taste rating (r = .55, 95% CI .44 to .64, p < .001, n = 193) and, negatively, with the wait at the last visit (r = −.30, p < .001, n = 178). In a regression on both, each point of taste was worth 0.81 points of satisfaction (95% CI 0.60 to 1.01) and each minute of waiting cost 0.13 (0.05 to 0.21), R² = .32, F(2, 175) = 41.5, p < .001, n = 178." A correlation is its own effect size, so the sentence needs no second one; a regression coefficient's unit is the sentence – *points of satisfaction per point of taste* – and the reader should be told the units rather than left to read B. Where a family of correlations was tested, say how many and which adjustment; where predictors were compared, β is the number that puts them on one scale and belongs in the sentence that compares them.

*A structure.* "The three food items formed a scale of acceptable consistency (Cronbach's α = .71, 95% CI .63 to .77; ω = .72) and the three service items a somewhat weaker one (α = .67, .58 to .74; ω = .67), with the reverse-keyed item recoded. An exploratory factor analysis (minimum residual, oblimin) recovered the two intended factors – parallel analysis retained two, loadings .59 to .76 on Food and .58 to .72 in absolute value on Service, the reverse-keyed item loading negatively, no cross-loading above .20, factor correlation .39 – and a two-factor confirmatory model fit the data, χ²(8) = 6.41, p = .60, CFI = 1.00, RMSEA = .000 (90% CI .000 to .071), SRMR = .034, where a one-factor model did not, Δχ²(1) = 57.7, p < .001." The reporting sections of the [latent variables](./latent-variables.md#reporting) page and the [factor](../factor-analysis.md#reporting-checklist) and [SEM and CFA](../structural-equation-modeling.md#reporting-a-cfa) manuals say which fit indices and which decisions – the extraction, the rotation, the rule for the number of factors – a reader needs to see.

## What the reader needs and what they do not

Three habits separate a report that can be checked from one that can only be believed.

*Estimate, interval, p – all three, every time.* A p on its own says whether; an estimate says what; an interval says how sure. The café's branch gap of 1.61 with an interval of 0.62 to 2.60 tells a reader more than p = 0.002 ever could, and a report that gives only the p has thrown away the two numbers that mattered. The same goes for a non-significant result: its interval is the finding.

*Exact numbers, sensibly rounded.* p to two or three decimals, never "p < 0.05" when the value is known, and "< 0.001" only when it is. Estimates to the precision the measurement had – a spend to the cent, a 0–10 score to one decimal – and effect sizes to two decimals. The app prints more decimals than a sentence should carry; rounding is the writer's job, and rounding a p of 0.051 to 0.05 is not rounding.

*The receipts.* Degrees of freedom with every statistic, the sample size with every result, and the notes the app printed under a table – a member excluded, a correction applied, an exact test used, a replicate count – in the Method or in a footnote. A reader who wants to reproduce the analysis needs the file, the fixes, the design and the settings; give those and they can. The app's [reproducibility](../comparison-analysis.md#reproducibility) sections say which R functions ran, and a project file saved from the app records every choice made.

And three things a report does not need: the group-by-group intervals when the interval on the difference is given; every effect size the app offers when one was chosen in advance; and the word *proven*, which no sample supports. The finding is what the data showed, stated with its uncertainty; the reader decides what it means.

## Where this appears in DataSuite

- Every analysis manual ends with a **Reporting checklist** in Method and Results halves – [comparison](../comparison-analysis.md#reporting-checklist), [correlation](../correlation-analysis.md#reporting-checklist), [regression](../regression-analysis.md#reporting-checklist), [reliability](../reliability-analysis.md#reporting-checklist), [factor analysis](../factor-analysis.md#reporting-checklist) and the rest – and a **Reproducibility** section naming the packages behind its tests.
- The [R console](../r-console.md) shows the code the app ran, for a Method appendix or for a reviewer.
- The citation card the app assembles under its output lists the packages to cite for the tests that ran.
- The concept pages each end with a reporting section for their own quantity: [a test](./hypothesis-testing.md#reporting-a-test), [an effect size](./effect-sizes.md#reporting-an-effect-size), [an interval](./confidence-intervals.md#reporting-an-interval), [a distribution](./distributions.md#reporting-a-distribution), [an assumption](./assumptions.md#reporting), [an outlier or a missing value](./outliers-missing-data.md#reporting), [a regression](./regression-basics.md#reporting), [a latent-variable model](./latent-variables.md#reporting), [a power calculation](./power-sample-size.md#reporting-a-plan).
