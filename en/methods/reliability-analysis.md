# Reliability analysis – method notes

The decisions behind the [reliability analysis](../reliability-analysis.md) module – which estimator produces each coefficient, how its interval is built, where its thresholds come from, what happens when a fit fails, and the numbers that validate them, each computed on the example dataset ([`cafe.csv`](../../examples/cafe.csv)). Sections follow the manual's.

## Reverse-scored items

**Reversal mirrors around the declared range, falling back per side to the range observed across the whole scale.** A reversed item becomes lowest + highest − old. If the user types both endpoints into **Response scale range** those are used; a blank side takes the extreme observed across *all* selected items, not the item's own. Reversing each item around its own observed range would put differently-answered items on different scales – an item nobody scored 1 on would be mirrored around 2–5 and shifted a point against the rest – and the shift would surface as a spurious item-mean difference. Pooling keeps items sharing one response format comparable when the endpoints are not declared; declaring them is still the right thing to do, since the pooled range can also fall short of the questionnaire's when no member used an endpoint on any item.

**Items keyed against the scale are surfaced, never auto-reversed.** `psych::alpha()` defaults to `check.keys = TRUE`, which silently flips any item that correlates negatively with the total. The module passes `check.keys = FALSE` to every psych call and instead runs the keying detection on the scored data – after the reverse-scored list is applied – reporting any item it still reads as keyed against the rest in a note under the metrics table and as a **Mark this item for reverse scoring** action on the item's row. A silent flip would hide the most common scoring error – a reverse-keyed item left unticked – behind a plausible alpha; the note makes the user decide, and the action makes deciding cheap. Detection rather than the raw rest correlation, because on a balanced scale – as many items keyed each way – the raw total cancels and the rest correlations name items almost at random.

**Detect is the IRT module's rule, run per scale.** The picker's **Detect** button and the note above share one routine with the IRT view – a principal-component start, items flipped until no rest correlation on the rescored total is negative, a 2/√n band for the unclassifiable, the majority's direction kept – whose reasoning, validation and acquiescence limit are in the [IRT method notes](./irt-analysis.md#b-detect). With several scales each is detected on its own items, since an item's keying is relative to the scale it is read in; an item two scales key differently cannot be ticked once for both – an item's reversal applies in every scale holding it – so it is left unselected and named. {#detect}

*Validation.* On the café's six satisfaction items with `slow` unreversed, **Detect** selects `slow`, and alpha with it ticked, 0.70286, matches a run on data with `slow` reversed beforehand; the note names `slow` only while it is left unticked.

**Items without variance are withheld before the fit.** psych's estimators throw on a constant column ("missing values (NAs) in the correlation matrix"), and one such item would take every coefficient in the run down with it. The module drops any item with fewer than two distinct non-missing values after reverse scoring and listwise deletion, lists it in the card's scale information as excluded for no variance, and runs on the rest; a scale left with fewer than two items is skipped and named.

## Reliability metrics

**Alpha's interval is Feldt's F interval; every other coefficient is bootstrapped.** Feldt (1965) gives α's sampling distribution under compound symmetry as a transformed F with n − 1 and (n − 1)(k − 1) degrees of freedom, so the interval is $1 - (1 - \alpha)F$ at the two tail quantiles, computed on the complete cases. It is exact under tau-equivalence, approximate otherwise, instant, and bounded below 1 where the symmetric $\alpha \pm z \cdot \text{SE}$ interval is not. Standardized alpha takes the same form, since raw and standardized α coincide under compound symmetry. None of the other coefficients has a usable closed-form interval, so they share [one bootstrap](#b-the-bootstrap-is-the-bias-corrected-percentile-interval-without-acceleration). {#confidence-intervals-for-reliability-estimates #ci}

**The bootstrap is the bias-corrected percentile interval, without acceleration.** Reliability coefficients are bounded above by 1 and their sampling distributions are left-skewed, which is exactly where the plain percentile interval undercovers; the BC correction shifts the quantiles by the bias between the point estimate and the median of the replicates. Acceleration (BCa) is left out because its jackknife would cost n further refits per coefficient. Each replicate resamples cases with replacement and refits the four estimators once, reading every selected coefficient off that one refit, so adding a coefficient to a run costs little; the replication count is the [bootstrap replications](../settings.md#bootstrap-replications) setting and the stream is the [bootstrap seed](../settings.md#bootstrap-seed). An interval is left blank when fewer than half the replicates produced a value – a coefficient that fails on most resamples has no interval worth printing. `omega()` and `glb.fa()` are refit on every replicate, which is why enabling intervals with those two selected is slow on larger scales.

**Omega comes from a bifactor solution sized by the item count.** `psych::omega()` is called with `nfactors = max(1, min(3, k ÷ 3))` – one group factor per three items, at most three, maximum-likelihood extraction, `flip = FALSE` so the module's own keying stands. Below six items the bifactor model is under-identified, so the call degenerates to the congeneric single-factor solution: ω total is reported and ω hierarchical is not defined, and a note under the table says so. The same factor count is reused for the drop table and every bootstrap replicate, so every ω on the card comes from one estimator and the "if deleted" column compares like with like. {#mcdonalds-omega-ω #mcdonalds-ω-total #mcdonalds-ω-hierarchical #ω #ωh}

**Mean split-half is the Rulon–Flanagan coefficient averaged over 10,000 random splits.** `psych::splitHalf()` computes $1 - \operatorname{var}(\text{difference of halves})/\operatorname{var}(\text{total})$ for each split rather than the Spearman–Brown stepped-up correlation, and draws random splits once the item count makes enumeration impractical (from 17 items up); `n.sample` is pinned to psych's default of 10,000 so the point estimate, the drop table and the bootstrap replicates all draw the same number of splits, and the draw runs under the app's seed so a seeded run reproduces. With an even number of items the mean over all splits is algebraically identical to standardized alpha – averaging Rulon–Flanagan over every split is the same computation from another direction – so selecting both is a consistency check, not independent evidence. {#mean-split-half #split-half-reliability #split-half}

**λ2 and λ4 come from the split-half fit, λ6 from alpha().** `splitHalf()` returns Guttman's λ2 as `lambda2` and the maximum split-half as `maxrb`, which is λ4; Revelle's β is its `minrb`. λ6 is `alpha()`'s `G6(smc)`, the squared-multiple-correlation form. The three lambdas therefore depend on two different estimators, and a failure of one leaves the other's lambda in place. {#guttmans-lambda-λ2-λ4-λ6 #guttmans-lambda-2-λ2 #guttmans-lambda-4-λ4 #guttmans-lambda-6-λ6 #λ2 #λ4 #λ6 #revelles-beta-β #β}

**Composite reliability and AVE come from a one-factor maximum-likelihood fit.** `psych::fa(nfactors = 1, fm = "ml", rotate = "none")` supplies the standardized loadings; CR is $\frac{(\sum\lambda_i)^2}{(\sum\lambda_i)^2 + \sum(1 - \lambda_i^2)}$ and AVE is the mean squared loading. On a unidimensional scale CR reproduces McDonald's ω to the third decimal, because the two are the same coefficient from two parameterisations; the module keeps both because the CFA literature asks for CR by name. {#composite-reliability-cr #cr}

*Validation.* On the café's food items CR and ω both read 0.718.

**Coefficient H pins communalities below 1.** Hancock and Mueller's (2001) $H = \bigl(1 + \bigl(\sum h_i/(1 - h_i)\bigr)^{-1}\bigr)^{-1}$ divides by $1 - h_i$ for each item's communality $h_i$, which is infinite at a Heywood case (a loading of 1 or more). Communalities are capped at 0.9999 before the sum, so a Heywood item yields a finite, near-1 H rather than an error, and the card's loadings show where it came from. {#coefficient-h #h}

**GLB fails alone, like the four estimators below.** `psych::glb.fa()` fits a factor solution of its own and can error on data the other estimators handle, most often on very short scales – deleting an item from a three-item scale leaves two, on which it usually fails. The call is wrapped as the other fits are: an error leaves the row on the card with **Estimation failed** in place of a number and puts GLB in the failed-estimators note with R's reason, while a failure on one item's refit blanks only that "if deleted" cell rather than dropping the column. {#greatest-lower-bound-glb #glb}

**Each of the four estimators fails alone.** The coefficients come from four separate fits – `alpha()`, `fa()`, `splitHalf()` and `omega()` – and each is wrapped so that an error records the estimator's key and R's message and returns nothing. Every coefficient is gated on its estimator's fit: those that depend on a failed one keep their rows with **Estimation failed** in place of a value, and a note under the table names the estimator, lists what it cost (alpha: α, λ6 and the item-total correlations; the factor analysis: CR, AVE and H; split-half: the mean split-half, λ2, λ4 and β; omega: ω) and gives R's reason. Nothing silently disappears, and nothing that did not depend on the failure is withheld. The cell says *Estimation failed* rather than *Failed to converge* because none of these psych estimators stops on a convergence check: what fails is an error – a singular matrix, too few items, a refusal inside the package – and calling it non-convergence would report a failure the fit never had, so the note carries the error itself. A refit that errors only in the drop table costs that one "if deleted" cell and does not list its estimator as failed, since every coefficient the card reports from the full scale stands.

**The verdict ladder is George and Mallery's, graded on the unrounded value.** It grades the coefficients that estimate reliability – α, standardized α, ω, CR, the mean split-half, λ2 and λ6: Excellent from 0.90, Good above 0.80, Acceptable above 0.70, Questionable above 0.60 and Poor above 0.50 – every boundary below the top strict, as George and Mallery (2003) give them – and the redundancy caution only above 0.95, the top of the range Terwee et al. (2007) accept for internal consistency. On this card the badge grades the coefficient unrounded, so the verdict belongs to the value and not to the display precision, which a settings change re-renders without re-running; where rounding carries the printed number across a boundary from its verdict – 0.8996 printing 0.900 beside *Good* – the cell carries a ⁻ or ⁺ and its tooltip the exact value, so the label and the number never seem to disagree unexplained. ω hierarchical, coefficient H, AVE, the corrected item-rest correlation and the mean inter-item correlation are graded the same way, each on its own rule (below). The ladder is shared by every reliability verdict in the app – this module, the ω table of factor analysis, IRT and SEM's factor reliability – and wherever a verdict is shown both works are cited; with interpretations switched off, neither is.

**With the CI on, a verdict names the bands at both ends of the interval.** Koo and Li (2016) read an ICC by its 95% confidence interval – the bands its two limits fall in rather than the point's – and the app reads every graded coefficient on this card the same way, through the [shared mechanism](./interpretation.md#b-the-band-reads-the-interval-when-one-is-on-screen). No source reads a reliability coefficient's interval so, which is why a note under the table says the reading is Koo and Li's for the ICC, extended by the app, and a second names the level when it is not 95%, the level their reading is of; Koo and Li are cited whenever such a verdict renders. ω hierarchical has no interval and keeps its point's band.

**λ4, GLB and β carry no adequacy verdict.** The 0.50 ⁄ 0.60 ⁄ 0.70 ⁄ 0.80 ⁄ 0.90 bands in the **Interpretation** column were derived for α and ω as estimates of reliability. λ4 and GLB are *bounds* on reliability – the best split and the largest value the covariance matrix admits – and both capitalise on chance in small samples with many items; β measures general-factor saturation, a different quantity, and no source gives it bands of its own. Reading any of them against the same bands would call a bound "excellent" or a saturation index "poor" for reasons that have nothing to do with the scale. They print an em dash instead, and a note under the table says which coefficients and why.

**ω hierarchical and coefficient H read their own sources' cuts, not the reliability ladder.** Neither estimates the reliability of the total score: ω_h is the share of its variance due to the general factor, H the reliability of an optimally weighted composite, never below ω. For ω_h, Reise, Bonifay and Haviland (2013) tentatively propose a minimum above .50 and prefer values closer to .75, a guideline they call subjective: an exact 0.50 stays *Weak general factor*, as their "greater than" prints, and *Strong general factor* opening at 0.75 is the app's choice, since "closer to" sets no boundary. For H, Hancock and Mueller (2001, pp. 209–210) take the .70 that Nunnally and Bernstein recommend as minimum reliability as a reasonable goal, and .80 as a more stringent one; the *well-defined* reading is Rodriguez, Reise and Haviland's (2016), who print it as H above .80, so an exact 0.80 stays *Adequately defined construct*, a label that is the app's wording for the band from .70. George and Mallery's ladder would misread both – its *possible redundancy* rung above 0.95 means nothing for H, where higher is simply better. Both rules are the ones factor analysis and SEM read ([one rule per statistic](./interpretation.md#b-a-statistic-graded-in-two-modules-reads-one-rule)), so one value earns one word on every card.

**AVE is read on one cut, 0.50, and a low value is not alarmed.** Fornell and Larcker (1981) ask a factor to capture at least half its indicators' variance, so AVE reads *Adequate convergent validity* from 0.50 and, below it, is labelled by the bar itself, *Below .50*; no source sets a higher rung, and the card has none. The rule is shared with SEM's convergent-validity table. The 0.50 bar is demanding: values of 0.30 to 0.45 are common for well-established scales, because psychological constructs are broad and no single factor explains most of the variance in every item. When α and ω are solid, a low AVE means a broad construct rather than a bad scale, and the card therefore does not escalate it beyond the column label; the threshold carries its full weight only when discriminant validity is the question. {#average-variance-extracted-ave #ave}

## Output options

**Scale-level statistics are computed on complete cases only.** Under listwise deletion every case that reaches R is complete, so the choice costs nothing. Under pairwise deletion the upstream filter does not run, and a row summed across items with a missing item would be floor-summed – the missing item counted as zero – biasing the scale mean, SD and variance downward. The module sums only rows complete on every item, reports the count as **Cases with complete data** when it differs from the number of cases, and says so in a note under the table; item-level statistics use each item's own answered cases, as psych's pairwise correlations do. {#cases-with-complete-data}

**Floor and ceiling effects are read on the scale score, at the lowest and highest score the scale allows.** McHorney and Tarlov (1995) defined the effect on an instrument's scale score – more than 15% of respondents at the lowest or highest *possible* score – and Terwee et al. (2007) carry the 15% into their quality criteria for health-status questionnaires; neither sets a rule for single items. The module therefore takes the possible extremes as the number of items times the declared response bounds, counts the complete cases whose total lands on each, and grades the two shares strictly above 15%, unrounded, citing both works whenever the verdict is shown. Read at an item's own observed minimum, as an item-level check would, any item whose lowest used category held more than 15% would count as floored even when that category sits mid-scale. A bound left empty falls back to the most extreme response observed across the items, as reverse scoring does, so the check still runs; since the "possible" score is then only the observed one, the card's **Response range** line names the side taken from the data. {#at-the-lowest-possible-score #at-the-highest-possible-score}

**The item-total columns are psych's `raw.r`, `r.drop` and `r.cor`.** `raw.r` correlates the item with a total that includes it; `r.drop` correlates it with the sum of the other items, and is the classical corrected item-total correlation the discrimination cut-offs were defined for, so the **Interpretation** column reads it and nothing else. The cut-offs are 0.30, Nunnally and Bernstein's (1994) bar for a discriminating item, with a negative value their sign of a miskeyed one, and 0.20, below which Streiner, Norman and Cairney (2015) discard an item, leaving 0.20 to 0.30 marginal; no rung sits above adequate, since no source grades a "good" item-rest r – the 0.40 sometimes quoted is Ebel's, for the upper–lower D index of achievement tests – and both works are cited whenever the column is graded. `r.cor` additionally disattenuates for the rest-scale's unreliability and runs systematically higher – useful as an estimate of the item's correlation with the construct, not as a discrimination index, and meaningless when the scale's reliability is near zero or negative. psych computes it as the item's column sum over the square root of the scale's common variance (the correlation matrix with squared multiple correlations on the diagonal), so a mis-keyed scale collapses that denominator and every value in the column – not only those beyond ±1 – is computed on it; the module therefore withholds the whole column, with a note under the table, when any value is undefined or exceeds ±1, rather than blanking or clamping cells that would look valid. {#corrected-item-total-r-item-rest}

**A coefficient that would rise without the item is stated as a fact, not a recommendation.** Choosing deletions on "α if deleted" capitalises on chance: on most scales at least one item nudges some coefficient upward by a hair, which is sampling noise and does not replicate. The **Interpretation** column therefore states the rise neutrally – *α if deleted: 0.74, above the scale's α* – uncoloured and without the word *improve*, and only when the rise survives the active precision, whatever the mode, decimal places or significant figures, since a smaller one prints as zero and cannot be seen. For the same reason a row with nothing to report reads *No issues flagged* rather than *Good item*: an item that tripped no flag has not been shown to be good. {#item-analysis-interpretation}

**The drop table needs at least three items.** Deleting an item from a two-item scale leaves one, on which every internal-consistency coefficient is undefined, so **Reliability if item deleted** is skipped below three items and a note explains why. {#reliability-if-item-deleted}

**The item statistics carry no flag.** No published source sets a variance floor for a single item, so the mean, SD and N print without a verdict, as every band without a source is dropped ([no band without a source](./interpretation.md#b-no-band-without-a-source)); an item with no variance at all is still withheld before the fit (Reverse-scored items, above), and no source sets a floor or ceiling rule at item level either, so that check reads the scale score instead (above). {#item-statistics}

**The mean inter-item correlation, and every single one, is read on Clark and Watson's 0.15 to 0.50.** Clark and Watson (1995, p. 316) recommend a mean inter-item correlation between 0.15 and 0.50 – near 0.15–0.20 for a broad construct, 0.40–0.50 for a narrow one – and extend the range to virtually every individual correlation (2019, p. 10). The mean is graded against it and the legend under the scale statistics carries the breadth qualification, since where in the range a scale should sit depends on the construct; with interpretation on, each correlation of the matrix outside the range is marked too, two-item scales included, since the range applies to the single correlation as written. The printed range is closed, so 0.50 stays within it. Above it reads *Above the recommended range* rather than a redundancy warning: no source read gives the upper bound that meaning. {#mean-inter-item-correlation}

**Correlations are product-moment, whatever the items' declared type.** Every coefficient on the card is estimated from the Pearson correlation or covariance matrix, and when every item is typed ordinal a note under the inter-item matrix says so. What the card then reports is the reliability of the *summed item scores*, not of latent variables assumed behind the response categories. Polychoric-based alternatives exist and give higher numbers; whether they are the better estimate is actively disputed, so the module reports the product-moment estimate and names its basis rather than choosing a side. {#inter-item-correlation-matrix}

## Missing data

**Listwise deletion runs in the app; pairwise leaves the gaps to psych.** Under the [listwise setting](../settings.md#missing-data) the module drops every case with a missing value on any selected item before the data reach R, so every estimator sees a complete matrix and every table describes the same cases. Under pairwise, the incomplete rows are passed through: `alpha()`, `fa()` and the inter-item matrix work from pairwise-complete correlations, item statistics use each item's own cases, and the scale-level statistics fall back to complete cases (the note above). Imputation replaces the values before the module sees them. The two settings can give different coefficients on the same file whenever missingness is uneven across items, and the reporting checklist asks which was used for that reason.
